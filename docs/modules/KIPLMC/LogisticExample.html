The Bayesian logistic example has a Gaussian location prior and finitely many Bernoulli observations. The model is U(θ,x)=τ2∥x−θ∥2+∑j[log⁡(1+e⟨vj,x⟩)−yj⟨vj,x⟩]U(\theta,x)=\frac{\tau}{2}\|x-\theta\|^2+\sum_j[\log(1+e^{\langle v_j,x\rangle})-y_j\langle v_j,x\rangle]U(θ,x)=2τ​∥x−θ∥2+∑j​[log(1+e⟨vj​,x⟩)−yj​⟨vj​,x⟩], where τ=σ−2\tau=\sigma^{-2}τ=σ−2. Additive normalization constants do not affect any derivative. This chapter derives the joint gradient and its Fréchet derivative, proves the exact global smoothness constant printed in the paper, and proves convexity and the full-span strict-convexity statement. It also proves that no finite unregularized model in a nonzero latent dimension has a positive global strong-convexity constant. Consequently the experimental model illustrates the algorithms but does not automatically satisfy the hypotheses of the quantitative convergence theorems.

The full joint derivatives distinguish global smoothness, pointwise strict convexity, and uniform strong convexity. Common-translation directions prove that finite unregularized logistic models need additional regularization for the global quantitative convergence assumptions.

03Find the exact Hessian nullspace §

Mathematical statement and proof

Every term in the second variation is nonnegative when τ≥0\tau\ge0τ≥0. If τ>0\tau>0τ>0, a zero total forces w−u=0w-u=0w−u=0 and ⟨vj,w⟩=0\langle v_j,w\rangle=0⟨vj​,w⟩=0 for every feature. Conversely those equations make every term vanish. The proof isolates the nonnegative prior term, bounds each observation term by the whole sum, and uses strict positivity of every logistic weight to cancel it. If the features span the latent space, orthogonality to every feature extends to their linear span by submodule induction. Taking the inner product with www itself then gives w=0w=0w=0, and hence u=0u=0u=0. Every nonzero joint direction therefore has strictly positive second variation at each finite state.

Because every finite predictor has a positive logistic weight, zero observation curvature means orthogonality to every feature. Full span eliminates that nullspace at each finite state. This is a pointwise positive-definiteness statement; it does not provide a positive lower eigenvalue uniformly over all states.

05Use the Euclidean norm for the joint gradient §

Mathematical statement and proof

The source norm is the Euclidean product norm ∥(θ,x)∥2=∥θ∥2+∥x∥2\|(\theta,x)\|^2=\|\theta\|^2+\|x\|^2∥(θ,x)∥2=∥θ∥2+∥x∥2. The joint state is equipped with this Euclidean Hilbert norm for the gradient claims. The coordinate equivalence is continuous and linear. Differentiating the prior and all finite observations proves Fréchet differentiability. The explicit gradient is (−τ(x−θ),  τ(x−θ)+∑j[s(⟨vj,x⟩)−yj]vj)(-\tau(x-\theta),\;\tau(x-\theta)+\sum_j[s(\langle v_j,x\rangle)-y_j]v_j)(−τ(x−θ),τ(x−θ)+∑j​[s(⟨vj​,x⟩)−yj​]vj​). To identify it with the Hilbert gradient, compare the Fréchet derivative on an arbitrary direction with the directional derivative above. Expanding the product inner product gives the same functional, so uniqueness of derivatives proves the gradient formula. The resulting equality is with the gradient, not a separately named surrogate force.

The Euclidean joint norm is needed because the gradient is defined through its inner product with arbitrary joint directions. A maximum product norm would not give that Hilbert gradient interpretation. The explicit formula therefore uses the same geometry as the smoothness and Hessian statements.
