Construction of Gaussian coordinates with Brownian covariance

In the time Hilbert space L2(R,dt)L^2(\mathbb R,dt)L2(R,dt), define et=1(0,t]e_t=1_{(0,t]}et​=1(0,t]​ for t≥0t\ge0t≥0. Then ⟨es,et⟩=min⁡(s,t)\langle e_s,e_t\rangle=\min(s,t)⟨es​,et​⟩=min(s,t). Applying the isonormal map gives centered Gaussian variables BtB_tBt​ with this covariance. Linearity gives joint Gaussianity, and disjoint increment indicators are orthogonal, so the corresponding Gaussian increments are independent.

Independent product copies provide the spatial coordinates. Fixed-time representatives are measurable and retain the stated Gaussian laws. The conclusions concern finite-dimensional distributions and increments; sample-path continuity is not asserted. The later integral equation uses continuity in the mean-square norm, proved from the increment moments.

01The time-function Hilbert space §

Mathematical statement and proof

Take the deterministic Hilbert space L2(R,dt)L^2(\mathbb R,dt)L2(R,dt). Interval indicators are its vectors, and the isonormal covariance formula evaluates their inner products. It is distinct from the random-variable L2L^2L2 space on the Gaussian source.

The time-index Hilbert space uses Lebesgue square integration on the real line. Indicators of finite intervals belong to it because their squared integral is the interval length.

02An interval indicator as a time index §

Mathematical statement and proof

Let et=1(0,t]e_t=\mathbf1_{(0,t]}et​=1(0,t]​ as a class in L2(dt)L^2(dt)L2(dt), for t≥0t\ge0t≥0. Construct it with the square-integrable interval indicator and the finite Lebesgue measure of (0,t](0,t](0,t]. Endpoints do not affect this Lebesgue L2L^2L2 class, but this half-open convention makes interval intersection identities direct.

The indicator is one before time ttt and zero afterward, up to sets of Lebesgue measure zero. Its squared Hilbert norm is ttt, so the associated isonormal variable has variance ttt.

03The overlap inner product of two indicators §

Mathematical statement and proof

The product of the two indicators is the indicator of [0,min⁡(s,t)][0,\min(s,t)][0,min(s,t)] almost everywhere. Integrating it gives ⟨es,et⟩=min⁡(s,t)\langle e_s,e_t\rangle=\min(s,t)⟨es​,et​⟩=min(s,t). The proof evaluates the Lebesgue measure of that finite interval, giving the deterministic inner product used in the Gaussian covariance calculation.

The product of the two indicators is the indicator of their overlap. Its integral is min⁡(s,t)\min(s,t)min(s,t), yielding the desired covariance kernel exactly.

04A countable basis index §

Mathematical statement and proof

Choose an orthonormal basis of the time-function Hilbert space and use its index set to label the independent scalar Gaussian coordinates. Orthogonal-series synthesis depends only on square-summable coefficients, so its definition does not require fixing an enumeration.

A countable basis index permits synthesis from a countable Gaussian product source. The particular enumeration affects the construction's description but not its covariance or resulting Gaussian laws.

05An orthonormal basis for time functions §

Mathematical statement and proof

Select a Hilbert basis for the Hilbert space of time functions by Hilbert-space basis existence. This basis supplies the coordinate isometry for the explicit isonormal Gaussian family; all subsequent covariance identities follow from isometry and do not depend on which basis was selected.

An orthonormal basis expands each time indicator with square-summable coefficients. Parseval identifies their squared sum with the interval length, ensuring the Brownian synthesis has the correct variance.

06The scalar Gaussian sample space §

Mathematical statement and proof

A sample is a real family indexed by the chosen time-Hilbert basis. Equip this product space with the independent standard Gaussian coordinate measure used in the isonormal series.

The sample space is the Gaussian coordinate source underlying the isonormal map. Time is an index of synthesized variables on this fixed space, rather than an additional random coordinate.

07The scalar source probability §

Mathematical statement and proof

Take the product of standard scalar Gaussian measures indexed by the chosen time basis. This probability has independent coordinate projections, providing the source variables used to synthesize every time value on one common sample space.

The scalar source probability has mass one. All synthesized time values are therefore random variables under one probability law, permitting their finite-dimensional covariances to be compared.

10The scalar Gaussian process §

Mathematical statement and proof

At each time select the strongly measurable representative of a brownian value as a square-integrable variable. This defines a measurable random variable at each time, written Bt(ω)B_t(\omega)Bt​(ω) for use in the stated pushforward law and finite-dimensional distributions. Equalities of L2L^2L2 classes still translate to almost-everywhere equalities of these representatives.

Choose the prescribed representative of each L2L^2L2 time value to define the scalar process. Fixed-time laws are unaffected by the representative choice on null sets.

The isonormal map is centered at every index vector. Applying it to a time indicator therefore gives zero mean for each process value.

13The Brownian covariance formula §

Mathematical statement and proof

The isonormal covariance formula gives Cov⁡(I(es),I(et))=⟨es,et⟩\operatorname{Cov}(I(e_s),I(e_t))=\langle e_s,e_t\rangleCov(I(es​),I(et​))=⟨es​,et​⟩. Substitute the overlap formula ⟨1(0,s],1(0,t]⟩=min⁡(s,t)\langle1_{(0,s]},1_{(0,t]}\rangle=\min(s,t)⟨1(0,s]​,1(0,t]​⟩=min(s,t) to obtain min⁡(s,t)\min(s,t)min(s,t). This calculation establishes the Brownian covariance for the chosen product probability and representatives.

Writing Bt=I(et)B_t=I(e_t)Bt​=I(et​), the centered variables therefore satisfy E[BsBt]=min⁡(s,t)\mathbb E[B_sB_t]=\min(s,t)E[Bs​Bt​]=min(s,t). The expectation is finite by Cauchy–Schwarz and the square integrability of the two variables.

14Joint Gaussianity of the time family §

Mathematical statement and proof

The isonormal isometry is linear and every output is scalar Gaussian. Apply Gaussianity of linear combinations of the isonormal map to the family t↦ett\mapsto e_tt↦et​. Every finite collection of the selected random variables is therefore jointly Gaussian.

Finite linear combinations of time values are isonormal evaluations at finite linear combinations of indicators. Their Gaussian laws establish the process's joint Gaussian property.

15The Brownian finite-dimensional and increment properties §

Mathematical statement and proof

A centered jointly Gaussian family with covariance min⁡(s,t)\min(s,t)min(s,t) has Brownian finite-dimensional distributions. Differences over disjoint intervals have zero covariance, so their joint Gaussianity makes them independent. These are the increment and distribution properties required by the square-mean construction; sample-path continuity is not asserted here.

Centered Gaussianity and covariance min⁡(s,t)\min(s,t)min(s,t) determine the Brownian finite-dimensional laws. Orthogonality of disjoint indicator increments gives independence of disjoint time increments.

19Brownian properties of each spatial coordinate §

Mathematical statement and proof

The product coordinate projection has the scalar Brownian source as its law. Pull the scalar pre-Brownian process back along this projection; its finite-dimensional distributions, mean, and covariance are preserved. Thus each vector coordinate has the established scalar Brownian laws.

The coordinate projection preserves the scalar probability law. Hence all centered Gaussian increment and covariance properties transfer to the corresponding spatial coordinate.

20Independence of the coordinate paths §

Mathematical statement and proof

The product coordinate projections are independent as sample-valued maps. Compose each projection with the map sending a scalar sample to its entire time-indexed process. Independence is preserved under these coordinatewise functions, giving independence of the whole coordinate paths.

Independence is asserted for whole coordinate paths as observations of separate product factors. It therefore entails independence of every finite collection of observations drawn from distinct coordinates.

21Independence after restriction to mesh times §

Mathematical statement and proof

Restrict each independent coordinate path to the deterministic grid nhnhnh. This is a coordinatewise function of the path, so independence is preserved. The result supplies exactly the countable grid-path hypothesis used in the innovation theorems.

Restricting each path to mesh times is a measurable observation of that path. Independence is preserved by this restriction, so the grid paths retain spatial independence.

22Normalization of the vector probability §

Mathematical statement and proof

Each scalar Brownian sample source has mass one. The infinite product over vector coordinates therefore also has mass one. This normalization concerns the vector product measure used to define the coordinate processes.

Normalization of the vector product source supplies the probability setting for the later Euclidean Gaussian and Brownian moment calculations. No additional weighting of the coordinate paths is introduced.
