01The projected root mean square error §

Mathematical statement and proof

Define the extended projected error RT,b(μ)=∥x↦Tx−b∥L2(μ)R_{T,b}(\mu)=\|x\mapsto Tx-b\|_{L^2(\mu)}RT,b​(μ)=∥x↦Tx−b∥L2(μ)​. Using an extended norm retains infinity for a divergent second moment and allows the following transport comparison before any finiteness assumption.

This quantity is an error about the specified deterministic center bbb, rather than a variance about the mean. It can therefore represent a parameter estimation error about a marginal mode. Keeping the extended integral initially allows the comparison to prove, rather than assume, its finiteness.

02Its finite ordinary value §

Mathematical statement and proof

Whenever the projected displacement has finite second moment, its projected root mean square radius is the ordinary finite number RT,b(μ)=(∫∥Tx−b∥2dμ)1/2R_{T,b}(\mu)=(\int\|Tx-b\|^2d\mu)^{1/2}RT,b​(μ)=(∫∥Tx−b∥2dμ)1/2. The transport and reference-radius bounds below establish this finiteness before the radius is used in a numerical inequality.

Once ∫∥Tx−b∥2dμ<∞\int\|Tx-b\|^2d\mu<\infty∫∥Tx−b∥2dμ<∞ is proved, its nonnegative square root is an ordinary real number. All later real-valued estimates use this finite case; a divergent second moment is never assigned a finite statistical-error interpretation.

03The pointwise projected difference bound §

Mathematical statement and proof

If ∥T∥≤1\|T\|\le1∥T∥≤1, then ∥T(x−y)∥≤∥x−y∥\|T(x-y)\|\le\|x-y\|∥T(x−y)∥≤∥x−y∥ pointwise. Monotonicity of the extended L2L^2L2 norm bounds the projected difference norm by the original coupling cost.

Squaring the pointwise contraction, integrating under an arbitrary coupling, and taking nonnegative square roots gives ∥T(X−Y)∥L2≤∥X−Y∥L2\|T(X-Y)\|_{L^2}\le\|X-Y\|_{L^2}∥T(X−Y)∥L2​≤∥X−Y∥L2​. No relation between the projected coordinates and the unobserved coordinates is required.

04Projected error bounded by a coupling §

Mathematical statement and proof

On a coupling write Tx−b=T(x−y)+(Ty−b)Tx-b=T(x-y)+(Ty-b)Tx−b=T(x−y)+(Ty−b). Minkowski bounds its L2L^2L2 norm by the sum of the two norms; the contraction estimate controls the first by coupling cost. Marginal identities rewrite the outer norms as RT,b(μ)R_{T,b}(\mu)RT,b​(μ) and RT,b(π)R_{T,b}(\pi)RT,b​(π).

The decomposition is valid because TTT is linear. The second term depends only on the reference marginal, so its value is the same for every coupling. This uniformity is precisely what lets the next step minimize only the full-state coupling cost.

05Projected error bounded by Wasserstein distance §

Mathematical statement and proof

The preceding bound holds for every coupling, with the same equilibrium radius. Take the infimum of coupling costs to obtain RT,b(μ)≤W2(μ,π)+RT,b(π)R_{T,b}(\mu)\le W_2(\mu,\pi)+R_{T,b}(\pi)RT,b​(μ)≤W2​(μ,π)+RT,b​(π). The family of couplings is nonempty under the stated hypotheses; no minimizing coupling is assumed.

For any ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, choose a coupling whose cost is within ε\varepsilonε of the finite transport infimum. The couplingwise inequality then bounds the projected error by W2+εW_2+\varepsilonW2​+ε plus the reference radius. Sending ε↓0\varepsilon\downarrow0ε↓0 gives the claimed estimate without an optimizer.

06Finiteness of projected error §

Mathematical statement and proof

Finite transport guarantees a coupling exists and makes W2(μ,π)W_2(\mu,\pi)W2​(μ,π) finite. The assumed equilibrium radius has a finite real upper bound. The transport-radius inequality therefore bounds the current projected norm by a sum of two finite extended values, proving its finiteness.

The argument supplies the projected moment even if no full second-moment bound for the first law has been stated separately. It needs only finite quadratic transport and a finite reference projected radius. This is useful when the statistical quantity is lower-dimensional than the full sampling state.

07The ordinary squared-moment formula §

Mathematical statement and proof

The finite projected square norm and measurability give x↦Tx−b∈L2(μ)x\mapsto Tx-b\in L^2(\mu)x↦Tx−b∈L2(μ). Its norm is therefore exactly ∫∥Tx−b∥2dμ\sqrt{\int\|Tx-b\|^2d\mu}∫∥Tx−b∥2dμ​, a finite nonnegative number. Thus the projected radius estimate concerns a finite second moment.

Absolute integrability of the squared norm is established before using the ordinary integral. The identity then follows from ∥F∥L22=∫∥F∥2\|F\|_{L^2}^2=\int\|F\|^2∥F∥L22​=∫∥F∥2, with the nonnegative square root selecting the norm rather than its negative.

08Inserting a target second-moment bound §

Mathematical statement and proof

The supplied L2L^2L2 membership makes the squared-norm integral finite. Its bound by c2c^2c2, together with c≥0c\ge0c≥0, implies the root-mean-square norm is at most ccc. The same inequality holds when the radius is viewed as an extended nonnegative value.

If the reference second moment is bounded by a radius squared, the radius must be nonnegative to conclude the desired norm bound after taking roots. The degenerate choice c=0c=0c=0 forces the projection to equal the center almost everywhere.

09The finite projected error estimate §

Mathematical statement and proof

First prove current-radius finiteness using finite transport and the finite equilibrium bound. All quantities in the transport-radius inequality are now finite, so it gives RT,b(μ)≤W2(μ,π)+cR_{T,b}(\mu)\le W_2(\mu,\pi)+cRT,b​(μ)≤W2​(μ,π)+c as an inequality of real numbers.

The final bound separates two sources of error: transport measures how far the numerical full-state law is from the target, while ccc measures target fluctuations around the chosen center. Only the first term can decay through additional sampling iterations at a fixed target.
