Finite quadratic transport transfers second moments

The two step-size regimes and the coefficient 1.65 follow Theorem 1(a)–(b) of Dalalyan and Karagulyan (2019). Dalalyan (2017), Theorem 1 and Section 6, gives the earlier synchronous-coupling argument.

Finite W2W_2W2​ distance to a finite-second-moment law supplies the other second moment automatically. This removes a redundant integrability premise when passing an initial law to the L² formulation.

A finite-cost coupling transfers second moments through ∥x∥2≤2∥x−y∥2+2∥y∥2\|x\|^2\le2\|x-y\|^2+2\|y\|^2∥x∥2≤2∥x−y∥2+2∥y∥2. Thus if one marginal has finite second moment and the transport infimum is finite, a finite-cost coupling proves the other marginal has one as well. Conversely, the product coupling of two square-integrable probability measures has finite cost by the same inequality. Applying these statements to laws transfers the moment conclusion back to the underlying random vectors.

Statement and proof

01A finite transport distance supplies a second moment §

Mathematical statement and proof

Finite Wasserstein distance supplies a finite-cost coupling. On that coupling space the second coordinate is in L2L^2L2 by the reference moment, and the coordinate difference is in L2L^2L2 by finite coupling cost. Their sum is the first coordinate, so its law μ\muμ has a finite second moment. No optimal coupling is selected.

Explicitly, ∥x∥2≤2∥x−y∥2+2∥y∥2\|x\|^2\le2\|x-y\|^2+2\|y\|^2∥x∥2≤2∥x−y∥2+2∥y∥2. Integrating on a finite-cost coupling bounds the first marginal's second moment by two finite quantities. Thus finiteness of transport to one finite-moment reference determines the ordinary second-moment class of the other marginal.

02Second moments give a finite transport distance §

Mathematical statement and proof

Use the explicit independent coupling μ⊗π\mu\otimes\piμ⊗π. Each coordinate is in L2L^2L2, hence their difference is in L2L^2L2 and has finite cost. Wasserstein distance is at most this coupling cost, proving finiteness.

For independent X∼μX\sim\muX∼μ and Y∼πY\sim\piY∼π, the same squared triangle inequality gives E∥X−Y∥2≤2E∥X∥2+2E∥Y∥2\mathbb E\|X-Y\|^2\le2\mathbb E\|X\|^2+2\mathbb E\|Y\|^2E∥X−Y∥2≤2E∥X∥2+2E∥Y∥2. Taking a square root supplies an explicit finite admissible cost, hence an upper bound for the transport infimum.

03Transferring the moment statement through a law §

Mathematical statement and proof

The finite-transport theorem first proves id∈L2(μ)\mathrm{id}\in L^2(\mu)id∈L2(μ). Replace μ\muμ by the supplied pushforward law of XXX, then use the change-of-law L2L^2L2 criterion to pull this moment back to XXX on its original sample space.

The law change is the identity ∫∥x∥2dμ(x)=E∥X∥2\int\|x\|^2d\mu(x)=\mathbb E\|X\|^2∫∥x∥2dμ(x)=E∥X∥2. It transfers the moment conclusion to the original random variable without constructing a new realization or asserting any independence of that realization from other variables on its space.

04The moment implication for pushforward laws §

Mathematical statement and proof

Specialize the preceding theorem to μ=P∘X−1\mu=P\circ X^{-1}μ=P∘X−1, whose law identity is reflexive. Finite transport of the actual pushforward to a finite-moment reference therefore gives X∈L2(P)X\in L^2(P)X∈L2(P).

The statement applies to the actual measured random vector, not merely to an abstract law with the same name. Measurability supplies the pushforward change of variables, while finite transport and the reference moment supply finiteness of the resulting squared-norm integral.
