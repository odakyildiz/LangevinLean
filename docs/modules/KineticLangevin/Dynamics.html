Existence for the kinetic integral equation

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L² on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

For the existence argument, equip the phase product with the maximum norm. This norm is equivalent to the Euclidean phase norm but is used here only to establish a global Lipschitz vector field. The input noise is any continuous additive velocity path starting at zero. The theorem therefore constructs paths for each prescribed input. A Brownian solution additionally requires a probability law for that input.

The construction is pathwise and works for arbitrary continuous additive forcing. This provides the deterministic solution map on which the later Brownian law and coupling constructions are built.

02A Lipschitz bound on the phase drift §

Mathematical statement and proof

If ggg is LLL-Lipschitz and γ,u,L≥0\gamma,u,L\ge0γ,u,L≥0, write R=∥(v,x)−(w,y)∥max⁡R=\|(v,x)-(w,y)\|_{\max}R=∥(v,x)−(w,y)∥max​. Each coordinate difference is at most RRR, and ∥g(x)−g(y)∥≤LR\|g(x)-g(y)\|\le LR∥g(x)−g(y)∥≤LR. The velocity component of the negative drift is bounded by (γ+uL)R(\gamma+uL)R(γ+uL)R using the triangle inequality. The position component is bounded by RRR. Hence the maximum is bounded by (γ+uL+1)R(\gamma+uL+1)R(γ+uL+1)R. The argument expands the product norm maximum, proves the two coordinate bounds, rewrites the drift difference into scalar multiples of differences with vector-space algebra, and combines the nonnegative coefficients. This Lipschitz constant suffices for existence. The contraction rate will instead follow from the curvature assumptions.

The maximum norm is useful because each coordinate projection has operator norm one. A sharper phase Lipschitz constant would also prove existence, but its value is immaterial for the later contraction rate: that rate is derived separately from curvature in the transformed Hilbert norm.

03Finite-horizon existence for continuous forcing §

Mathematical statement and proof

Let the underlying normed space be complete, BBB continuous with B0=0B_0=0B0​=0, and T≥0T\ge0T≥0. The continuous-input Picard existence theorem gives a continuous phase path YYY, with Y0=initialY_0=\text{initial}Y0​=initial, satisfying the kinetic integral identity for every 0≤t≤T0\le t\le T0≤t≤T. Apply that theorem to the negative drift with the Lipschitz constant above and the forcing (Bt,0)(B_t,0)(Bt​,0), whose continuity follows from product continuity and whose value at zero follows from the zero initial forcing condition. This is finite-horizon pathwise existence; Gaussian stationarity, a Brownian input law, and convergence of the numerical chain are not conclusions of this theorem.

Picard iteration solves the integral equation on a short interval by contraction of the integral map. Global Lipschitz continuity allows successive intervals to be joined without finite-time blowup. On a prescribed finite horizon the resulting path is unique and depends continuously on the initial phase and the forcing.
