The quadratic force remainder from a Lipschitz Hessian

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2p\ge2p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L2L^2L2 on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

The second-order integrator linearizes the force at its initial position. Its error requires a quadratic estimate for this linearization, obtained from the explicitly integrated Hessian along a segment. The derivative and Hessian Lipschitz assumptions are ordinary analytic hypotheses on the force.

Hessian Lipschitzness controls the true force Taylor remainder by a quadratic displacement norm. The exact one-half factor comes from integrating the segment parameter, before any stochastic moment estimate is applied.

Statement and proof

01Integrate Hessian variation along the segment §

Mathematical statement and proof

The averaged Hessian is the Bochner integral of H(y+t(x−y))H(y+t(x-y))H(y+t(x−y)), with ttt between zero and one. Subtracting H(y)H(y)H(y) inside the integral gives an integrand with norm at most M2t∥x−y∥M_{2}t\|x-y\|M2​t∥x−y∥. The norm of an integral is at most the integral of this nonnegative upper bound, whose value is M2∥x−y∥/2M_{2}\|x-y\|/2M2​∥x−y∥/2. The argument checks continuity of the integrand, moves subtraction through the integral, applies the Lipschitz inequality on the segment, and evaluates the elementary integral of ttt.

Along the displacement segment, write the force change as the integral of its Hessian applied to the displacement. Subtract the Hessian frozen at the starting point inside that integral. Hessian Lipschitzness bounds the operator difference by the segment parameter times the displacement length.

02Apply the averaged operator to the displacement §

Mathematical statement and proof

The fundamental theorem of calculus identifies g(x)−g(y)g(x)-g(y)g(x)−g(y) with the averaged Hessian applied to x−yx-yx−y. Thus the true Taylor remainder equals (Hˉ−H(y))(x−y)(\bar H-H(y))(x-y)(Hˉ−H(y))(x−y). The operator norm inequality bounds this by ∥Hˉ−H(y)∥∥x−y∥\|\bar H-H(y)\|\|x-y\|∥Hˉ−H(y)∥∥x−y∥, yielding M2∥x−y∥2/2M_{2}\|x-y\|^{2}/2M2​∥x−y∥2/2. This is a remainder estimate; the Hessian is never replaced by an assumed scalar or a single intermediate point common to all vector coordinates.

Applying the resulting operator average to the displacement contributes a second displacement factor. Integration of the segment parameter gives one half. Thus the first-order Taylor remainder is bounded by M2∥x−y∥2/2M_2\|x-y\|^2/2M2​∥x−y∥2/2, with no probabilistic assumption.
