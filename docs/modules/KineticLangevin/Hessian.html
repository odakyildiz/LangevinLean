The averaged Hessian on a displacement segment

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2p\ge2p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L2L^2L2 on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

The mean Hessian is a Bochner integral of the continuous derivative of the force along the segment between two positions. The module derives its self-adjointness, lower and upper quadratic-form bounds and exact representation of the force difference. These properties establish that the force difference satisfies the operator equation used in the spectral contraction proof.

The averaged operator is not an arbitrary matrix satisfying spectral bounds. It is constructed from the Hessian along the displacement segment and is proved to reproduce the exact force difference.

01The segment average and its scalar pairings §

Mathematical statement and proof

Set Hˉ(x,y)=∫01H(y+t(x−y))dt\bar H(x,y)=\int_0^1H(y+t(x-y))dtHˉ(x,y)=∫01​H(y+t(x−y))dt. Continuity of HHH makes the operator-valued integrand continuous and integrable on the compact segment. A continuous linear map commutes with a Bochner integral, so applying the operator to www and pairing with zzz gives ⟨z,Hˉ(x,y)w⟩=∫01⟨z,H(y+t(x−y))w⟩dt\langle z,\bar H(x,y)w\rangle=\int_0^1\langle z,H(y+t(x-y))w\rangle dt⟨z,Hˉ(x,y)w⟩=∫01​⟨z,H(y+t(x−y))w⟩dt. First, the argument commutes operator evaluation through the interval integral, then commutes the continuous linear inner-product functional. The continuous linear operators form a Banach space because the underlying Hilbert space is complete.

The average is taken along the straight line joining the two positions. Pairing with fixed vectors turns the operator-valued integral into an ordinary scalar integral. This permits both symmetry and quadratic-form bounds to be through scalar identities on the segment.

02Symmetry and curvature bounds of the average §

Mathematical statement and proof

If every H(p)H(p)H(p) is self-adjoint, the pairing identity moves both sides into scalar integrals, where their integrands agree pointwise. If m∥z∥2≤⟨z,H(p)z⟩≤M∥z∥2m\|z\|^2\le\langle z,H(p)z\rangle\le M\|z\|^2m∥z∥2≤⟨z,H(p)z⟩≤M∥z∥2, integral monotonicity bounds the average between the integrals of these two constants. The interval has length one, leaving exactly the original constants. The argument supplies continuity and interval-integrability for each quadratic-form integrand and the constant comparison function, applies monotonicity of integration, and simplifies the constant integral. These results provide the symmetry and spectral premises needed by the transformed-energy proof.

For every vector uuu, integrate m∥u∥2≤⟨Hsu,u⟩≤M∥u∥2m\|u\|^2\le\langle H_su,u\rangle\le M\|u\|^2m∥u∥2≤⟨Hs​u,u⟩≤M∥u∥2 over an interval of length one. The bounds remain mmm and MMM. Symmetry is preserved because the equality of the two mixed pairings holds at each segment point.

03Representation of the force difference §

Mathematical statement and proof

Assume Dg(p)=H(p)Dg(p)=H(p)Dg(p)=H(p). The affine path ℓ(t)=y+t(x−y)\ell(t)=y+t(x-y)ℓ(t)=y+t(x−y) has derivative x−yx-yx−y, so the chain rule gives (g∘ℓ)′(t)=H(ℓ(t))(x−y)(g\circ\ell)'(t)=H(\ell(t))(x-y)(g∘ℓ)′(t)=H(ℓ(t))(x−y). Integrate this derivative from zero to one. The fundamental theorem yields ∫01H(ℓ(t))(x−y)dt=g(x)−g(y)\int_0^1H(\ell(t))(x-y)dt=g(x)-g(y)∫01​H(ℓ(t))(x−y)dt=g(x)−g(y). Commuting the operator evaluation with the average integral gives Hˉ(x,y)(x−y)=g(x)−g(y)\bar H(x,y)(x-y)=g(x)-g(y)Hˉ(x,y)(x−y)=g(x)−g(y). The argument constructs the affine-path derivative, composes the force derivative at each segment point, verifies integrability from continuity, and simplifies the endpoints. No coordinatewise mean-value theorem or unjustified single Hessian point is used.

Apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to s↦∇f(y+s(x−y))s\mapsto\nabla f(y+s(x-y))s↦∇f(y+s(x−y)). Its derivative is ∇2f(y+s(x−y))(x−y)\nabla^2f(y+s(x-y))(x-y)∇2f(y+s(x−y))(x−y). Integrating from zero to one gives the force difference, proving that the averaged operator is the coefficient in the synchronous difference equation.
