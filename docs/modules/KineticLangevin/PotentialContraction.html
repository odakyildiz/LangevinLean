Transformed contraction for a strongly convex smooth potential

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2p\ge2p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L2L^2L2 on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

A sector inequality for the gradient difference gives contraction without choosing a time-dependent averaged Hessian. Assume the potential is differentiable and strongly convex and has Lipschitz gradient. Its two-point strong-smooth inequality yields transformed dissipativity directly for the difference equation after common forcing cancels.

Integration gives the pathwise position estimate with factor 2e−mt/γ\sqrt2e^{-mt/\gamma}2​e−mt/γ in the stated friction regime. Construction of stochastic integral solutions and the infimum over initial probability couplings are used separately to obtain a Wasserstein estimate. No continuous Hessian is required for this argument.

01The energy derivative and the gradient sector inequality §

Mathematical statement and proof

For velocity difference vvv, position difference xxx, and force difference ggg, define D=2⟨v+γx,−g⟩+2⟨−v,γv+g⟩D=2\langle v+\gamma x,-g\rangle+2\langle-v,\gamma v+g\rangleD=2⟨v+γx,−g⟩+2⟨−v,γv+g⟩. The strong-smooth interpolation inequality is mM∥x∥2+∥g∥2≤(m+M)⟨g,x⟩mM\|x\|^2+\|g\|^2\le(m+M)\langle g,x\ranglemM∥x∥2+∥g∥2≤(m+M)⟨g,x⟩. Subtract the linear part mxmxmx, expand its squared norm, and rearrange to obtain ∥g−mx∥2≤(M−m)⟨x,g−mx⟩\|g-mx\|^2\le(M-m)\langle x,g-mx\rangle∥g−mx∥2≤(M−m)⟨x,g−mx⟩. The proof performs this expansion with the Hilbert norm-square and inner-product identities, then uses elementary real inequalities. This sector estimate contains the joint lower–upper curvature information needed for contraction.

Write the displacement as q=x−yq=x-yq=x−y and the force difference as d=∇f(x)−∇f(y)d=\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)d=∇f(x)−∇f(y). Strong convexity and smoothness place (q,d)(q,d)(q,d) in a sector narrower than a bare Lipschitz ball. This relation implies dissipation of the transformed energy.

02Complete a square to prove dissipativity §

Mathematical statement and proof

Put k=g−mxk=g-mxk=g−mx. Expanding the transformed energy yields

The potentially positive mixed term is paired with the negative position term and rewritten as a square plus a curvature contribution. The sector inequality controls the square. Using this relation retains the strong-convexity coefficient in the differential inequality.

03The sector condition from the potential §

Mathematical statement and proof

For g=∇f(x)−∇f(y)g=\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)g=∇f(x)−∇f(y) and displacement x−yx-yx−y, the strong-smooth interpolation inequality follows from differentiability, strong convexity and gradient Lipschitzness. the strong-smooth force dissipativity inequality applies that theorem, converts it to the sector condition, and applies the completed-square estimate to that sector inequality. Consequently no Hessian, sector bound, or contraction conclusion is assumed in this potential theorem. The explicit hypotheses retain m≤Mm\le Mm≤M, γ>0\gamma>0γ>0, and γ2≥m+M\gamma^2\ge m+Mγ2≥m+M.

One may apply the strong-smooth interpolation inequality directly to fff. No continuous Hessian is required: differentiability, strong convexity, and a Lipschitz gradient already imply the needed two-point estimate. Thus this proof applies to the regularity assumptions of the first-order sampling theorem.

04Differentiate the noise-cancelled path energy §

Mathematical statement and proof

Assume v′=−γv−gv'=-\gamma v-gv′=−γv−g and x′=vx'=vx′=v. Then (v+γx)′=−g(v+\gamma x)'=-g(v+γx)′=−g, while (−v)′=γv+g(-v)'=\gamma v+g(−v)′=γv+g. The argument obtains these two derivative formulas by addition, negation and constant scalar multiplication, normalizes them with vector-space algebra, and applies the squared-norm derivative rule to each coordinate. Their sum is precisely the energy derivative. The theorem assumes differentiability of the difference paths; it does not incorrectly differentiate an individual Brownian velocity path.

For two paths driven by identical forcing, subtract their integral equations first. Their position difference has derivative equal to their velocity difference, and their velocity difference has derivative equal to the negative friction and force difference. The energy chain rule is then an ordinary deterministic calculation.

06Use right derivatives at the initial time §

Mathematical statement and proof

A finite-horizon integral solution is constrained only for nonnegative time. Its extension to negative time need not have the same derivative. The correct initial derivative is therefore the derivative within the right half-line. The product and squared-norm derivative rules remain valid in this form, so differentiating the transformed energy gives the same force-energy expression. The argument applies those within-derivative rules to both transformed coordinates, cancels the opposing friction terms, and transfers the resulting derivative to the energy expression. The right-slope form of Gronwall requires exactly these hypotheses, and gives the same exponential energy decay throughout the closed interval. This is the version used by the integral-flow theorem.

At time zero all derivatives are interpreted from within the nonnegative time interval. This is sufficient for the integrating-factor argument on a closed finite horizon. It avoids extending either trajectory or its driving noise to negative times.
