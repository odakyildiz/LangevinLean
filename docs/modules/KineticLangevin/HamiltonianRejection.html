Quadratic rejection bounds under velocity and force second moments

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2p\ge2p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L2L^2L2 on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

The exact Verlet energy defect controls its Metropolis rejection probability. For steps between zero and one, the state-dependent bound simplifies to a single quadratic phase bound. Integrating this bound requires precisely the velocity and force second moments displayed in the theorem, and supplies the vanishing error needed for a fixed-horizon weak limit.

The numerical energy defect is reduced to an integrable quadratic phase bound. Its expectation gives a quantitative rejection bound, enabling exact corrected invariance to approximate uncorrected evolution.

01Apply the scalar rejection inequality to the energy defect §

Mathematical statement and proof

The acceptance is defined by the defect d=H(Vhz)−H(z)d=H(V_hz)-H(z)d=H(Vh​z)−H(z). The already proved scalar inequality 1−min⁡{1,e−d}≤∣d∣1-\min\{1,e^{-d}\}\le |d|1−min{1,e−d}≤∣d∣ therefore applies by substitution, with no acceptance approximation.

The scalar acceptance bound is first applied pointwise to the exact numerical energy change. An upper bound on its absolute value also bounds its positive part. The resulting rejection upper bound is measurable and can therefore be integrated under the target law.

The following theorem composes this estimate with the scalar rejection inequality.

Intermediate kick velocities and drift positions are bounded by the original velocity and force using the triangle inequality and force Lipschitzness. Squaring introduces cross terms, controlled by 2ab≤a2+b22ab\le a^2+b^22ab≤a2+b2. This reduces the upper bound to a finite sum of second moments.

03The integrated rejection bound §

Mathematical statement and proof

For any finite phase measure with integrable q(z)=∥v∥2+∥∇f(x)∥2q(z)=\|v\|^2+\|\nabla f(x)\|^2q(z)=∥v∥2+∥∇f(x)∥2, the rejection factor is measurable and lies between zero and one, so it is integrable. The pointwise bound integrates to ∫(1−ah) dπ≤h2M(2M+11)8∫q dπ.\int(1-a_h)\,d\pi\le h^2\frac{M(2M+11)}8\int q\,d\pi.∫(1−ah​)dπ≤h28M(2M+11)​∫qdπ. One proves bounded integrability of rejection directly from the acceptance bounds and applies monotonicity of the ordinary integral against the explicitly assumed finite quadratic moment. No one-step trajectory or stationary-flow conclusion is supplied as a premise.

Under a finite quadratic phase bound, the pointwise rejection estimate has a finite integral. The step-size factors remain explicit while the phase dependence becomes a target moment constant. This separation is what makes the later weak-error bound uniform over a fixed time horizon.
