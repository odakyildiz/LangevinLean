Finiteness and quadratic moments of the Hamiltonian Gibbs measure

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L² on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

The weak Hamiltonian limit needs finite mass and an integrable quadratic expression in velocity and force. This section derives both for the exponential Hamiltonian density in finite dimensions. It factors that density into a radial Gaussian velocity weight and the potential Gibbs weight, uses existing direct Gaussian-domination estimates, and transfers position moments to force moments by Lipschitz growth.

Strong convexity makes the Gibbs phase measure finite and gives the moments required by the rejection bound. Smoothness converts position control into force control, establishing the integrability assumptions of the weak limit.

Statement and proof

01Factor the Hamiltonian density §

Mathematical statement and proof

Because H(v,x)=f(x)+∥v∥2/2H(v,x)=f(x)+\|v\|^2/2H(v,x)=f(x)+∥v∥2/2, the positive exponential weight factors as e−H(v,x)=e−∥v∥2/2e−f(x)e^{-H(v,x)}=e^{-\|v\|^2/2}e^{-f(x)}e−H(v,x)=e−∥v∥2/2e−f(x). Therefore its product-Haar weighted measure is the product of the separately weighted velocity and position measures. The identity for products of density-weighted measures applies because the nonnegative density is the product of the two coordinate densities, as follows from the exponential addition identity. Normalization has not been assumed or imposed at this step.

The kinetic and potential parts of the Hamiltonian depend on different coordinates, so their exponential weights factor. Product integration separates velocity Gaussian integrals from position Gibbs integrals. This factorization is valid for the reference measure before normalization.

02Derive finite mass of both factors and their product §

Mathematical statement and proof

The velocity weight is the radial Gaussian with precision 1/21/21/2, whose ordinary integral is finite. The position weight has finite mass by the previously proved strong-convexity Gaussian domination: a supporting quadratic lower bound for fff makes e−fe^{-f}e−f integrable. The proof converts these finite ordinary integrals to finite extended density integrals, instantiates finite weighted measures, and uses the exact factorization to prove finiteness of the full phase measure.

Strong convexity gives a quadratic lower bound for the potential. Its exponential is dominated by a shifted Gaussian density, yielding finite positive partition mass. The velocity factor is an ordinary Gaussian integral, so the phase measure also has finite positive mass.

03Derive velocity and position second moments before normalization §

Mathematical statement and proof

A quadratic factor is absorbed by radial Gaussian decay, so ∫∥v∥2e−∥v∥2/2 dv<∞\int\|v\|^2e^{-\|v\|^2/2}\,dv<\infty∫∥v∥2e−∥v∥2/2dv<∞. The same strong-convexity tail estimate gives ∫∥x∥2e−f(x) dx<∞\int\|x\|^2e^{-f(x)}\,dx<\infty∫∥x∥2e−f(x)dx<∞. The argument uses the ordinary-integral characterization of integration under a density, checks that each exponential density is finite and measurable, and invokes the existing Gaussian-tail estimates. These are moments of the unnormalized factors, obtained without assuming stationarity of a process.

Polynomial factors remain integrable under the Gaussian density upper bound. Thus both velocity and position second moments exist before taking ratios by the partition function. Establishing this first ensures that later normalized moment expressions represent finite expectations.

04Transfer position moments to force moments §

Mathematical statement and proof

Gradient Lipschitz continuity implies ∥∇f(x)∥≤M∥x∥+∥∇f(0)∥\|\nabla f(x)\|\le M\|x\|+\|\nabla f(0)\|∥∇f(x)∥≤M∥x∥+∥∇f(0)∥. Squaring and using (a+b)2≤2a2+2b2(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2(a+b)2≤2a2+2b2 gives ∥∇f(x)∥2≤2M2∥x∥2+2∥∇f(0)∥2\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\le2M^2\|x\|^2+2\|\nabla f(0)\|^2∥∇f(x)∥2≤2M2∥x∥2+2∥∇f(0)∥2. The right-hand side is integrable under the finite position Gibbs weight by its second moment. The argument derives the force growth inequality from the Lipschitz difference bound and triangle inequality, then applies domination by this explicit integrable upper bound.

Force Lipschitzness gives ∥∇f(x)∥≤∥∇f(x0)∥+M∥x−x0∥\|\nabla f(x)\|\le\|\nabla f(x_0)\|+M\|x-x_0\|∥∇f(x)∥≤∥∇f(x0​)∥+M∥x−x0​∥. Squaring and using (a+b)2≤2a2+2b2(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2(a+b)2≤2a2+2b2 reduces force moments to the finite position second moment. Choosing a minimizer removes the constant force term when available.

05Assemble the precise phase bound needed by the weak limit §

Mathematical statement and proof

On the factored finite phase measure, ∥v∥2\|v\|^2∥v∥2 is integrable by pulling the velocity moment through the first coordinate, and ∥∇f(x)∥2\|\nabla f(x)\|^2∥∇f(x)∥2 is integrable by pulling the force moment through the second coordinate. Their sum is exactly the quadratic expression in the rejection bound. The argument rewrites the phase measure by its proved factorization, supplies finiteness of both factors, and adds these two coordinate integrability results. Thus every tail premise in the later Hamiltonian stationarity limit follows from positive strong convexity and a Lipschitz gradient.

The rejection upper bound is a fixed quadratic combination of velocity and force norms. The preceding moment bounds therefore make its target integral finite. This is the exact integrability required to make the accumulated rejection error vanish in the Hamiltonian-flow limit.
