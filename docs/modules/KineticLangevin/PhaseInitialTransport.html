Equality of initial position and common-velocity phase transport distances

References and publication status for this development

Published source; corrected checked proofs

The comparison uses the supplied mainDR_dec_18.tex manuscript. The results prove Wasserstein convergence of the kinetic diffusion, invariance of its Gibbs measure, KLMC and KLMC2 error estimates for correlated Gaussian increments, their Cholesky representations, and explicit iteration counts for a prescribed error. The corrected proofs are unpublished. The reference table records the correction to the continuous prefactor and the stochastic Hessian-difference terms retained in the second-order comparison. The KLMC2 estimate retains the exact fourth Gaussian moment in every dimension; the coarser displayed simplification assumes p≥2p\ge2p≥2. The stronger inverse-step-square exponential term is also proved. Continuous solutions are continuous in L2L^2L2 on finite time intervals. Almost-sure continuity of sample paths is not proved here.

  • Arnak S. Dalalyan and Lionel Riou-Durand (2020). On sampling from a log-concave density using kinetic Langevin diffusions. Bernoulli 26(3), 1956–1988. arXiv

The common initial velocity distribution is independent of each initial position distribution. Adjoining that identical velocity does not change their exact quadratic transport distance. This identifies the position-distance estimate with the initial phase-product distance printed in the source theorem, for the Brownian-driven solution.

Appending the same independent velocity gives an upper transport bound, while position projection gives the reverse bound. Together they identify the initial phase-product distance exactly with position Wasserstein distance.

Statement and proof

01The exact product transport identity §

Mathematical statement and proof

For the upper bound, couple the two initial positions and use the identical independent velocity in both phase states. Their phase displacement has zero velocity component, so its norm is exactly the original position displacement. The random-map transport theorem then gives the upper bound. For the reverse inequality, the position projection has Lipschitz constant one. Projecting every phase coupling therefore gives the position transport bound. The two extended-real inequalities prove exact equality, even before imposing finite moments. Real conversion preserves this equality.

For the lower bound, project any phase coupling onto positions. For the upper bound, extend any position coupling by one identical independent velocity. The added coordinate contributes zero cost. Taking both infima yields equality of the phase-product and position transport distances.

Earlier results used here: ULA-SC, Section 3.17.3; ULA-SC, Section 3.17.4.

02The source theorem’s literal full phase-product right-hand side §

Mathematical statement and proof

Apply the already proved specified Brownian mixing estimate, with arbitrary common finite-second-moment velocity distribution. The source restriction 0≤a<γ/20\le a<\gamma/20≤a<γ/2 gives the required positive denominator. Replacing the initial position Wasserstein distance by the exact common-velocity phase-product identity produces the source position-contraction formula with precisely its printed prefactor, exponent and initial phase-product distance. The Gibbs corollary specializes to Gaussian velocity and the proved stationary target, recovering the explicit 2e−mt/γ\sqrt2 e^{-mt/\gamma}2​e−mt/γ friction-regime statement with the literal initial Gaussian-times-position phase distance.

The theorem’s initial phase distribution has the same Gaussian velocity factor as the stationary target. The product transport identity therefore rewrites its phase distance exactly as the initial position distance. No extra velocity discrepancy is hidden in the printed right-hand side.

Earlier results used here: ULA-SC, Section 3.17.4; ULA-SC, Section 3.26.13; ULA-SC, Section 3.26.3.
