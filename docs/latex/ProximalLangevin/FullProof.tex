\chapter{Assumptions and main results}
\section{\texorpdfstring{MYIPLA: a finite original-\allowbreak{}model guarantee}{MYIPLA: a finite original-model guarantee}}


Write \(U=g_1+g_2\) on \(\mathbb R^{d_\theta+d_x}\). Assume \(g_1\) is differentiable, \(m\)-strongly convex for \(m>0\), and has \(L_1\)-Lipschitz gradient. Assume \(g_2\) is finite-valued, convex, bounded below, and \(K\)-Lipschitz. Define the actual proximal minimizer and Moreau envelope by


\[P_\eta(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p\left\{g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}\right\},\quad
g_2^\eta(x)=g_2(P_\eta(x))+\frac{\|P_\eta(x)-x\|^2}{2\eta},\]
\[V_\eta=g_1+g_2^\eta,\qquad L_\eta=L_1+\eta^{-1},\qquad\eta>0.\]


Take \(N\ge1\), \(d_\theta,d_x\ge0\), an L\ensuremath{{}^{2}} initial scaled particle state, and independent standard Gaussian innovations at all coordinates and times, independent of initialization. Write \(\rho_0\) for the initial scaled law, \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\) for the Gibbs target of the lifted \(V_\eta\), and choose marginal maximizers \(\theta_U\) for \(g_1+g_2\) and \(\theta_\eta\) for \(V_\eta\). Their existence is established in the supporting modules.



Precisely, the scaled state, lifted potential, and normalized target are


\[z=(\theta,x^1/\sqrt N,\ldots,x^N/\sqrt N),\qquad
W_{N,\eta}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^N V_\eta(\theta,\sqrt N z_i),\]
\[\Pi_{N,\eta}(dz)=Z_{N,\eta}^{-1}e^{-W_{N,\eta}(z)}dz.\]


A marginal maximizer of a pair potential \(V\) maximizes \(\theta\mapsto\int e^{-V(\theta,x)}dx\).



MYIPLA is the Gaussian IPLA recursion for \(V_\eta\), using the proved identity \(\nabla g_2^\eta=(I-P_\eta)/\eta\):


\[z_{n+1}=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla W_{N,\eta}(z_n)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\,\xi_n.\]


For \(0<\gamma\le2/(m+L_\eta)\) and every \(n\in\mathbb N\), its actual parameter iterate satisfies


\[\begin{aligned}
\bigl(\mathbb E\|\theta_n-\theta_U\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}
\le{}&\sqrt{\frac{\eta K^2}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}
+(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\eta})\\
&+\frac{33}{20}\frac{L_\eta}{m}\sqrt{d_x+\frac{d_\theta}{N}}\sqrt\gamma.
\end{aligned}\]


The terms are the finite smoothing displacement, sharp equilibrium radius, decaying initial transport error, and discretization bias. The smoothing rate is \(O(\sqrt\eta)\), and the discretization coefficient depends on \(\eta\). This is the proved corrected theorem; the paper’s Taylor-based linear smoothing expression is not asserted.

\section{\texorpdfstring{PIPGLA: an actual-\allowbreak{}law splitting bound}{PIPGLA: an actual-law splitting bound}}


Write \(U=g_1+g_2\) on \(\mathbb R^{d_\theta+d_x}\). Assume \(g_1\) is differentiable, \(m\)-strongly convex for \(m>0\), and has \(L_1\)-Lipschitz gradient. Assume \(g_2\) is finite-valued, convex, bounded below, and \(K\)-Lipschitz. Define the actual proximal minimizer and Moreau envelope by


\[P_\eta(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p\left\{g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}\right\},\quad
g_2^\eta(x)=g_2(P_\eta(x))+\frac{\|P_\eta(x)-x\|^2}{2\eta},\]
\[V_\eta=g_1+g_2^\eta,\qquad L_\eta=L_1+\eta^{-1},\qquad\eta>0.\]


Take \(N\ge1\), \(d_\theta,d_x\ge0\), an L\ensuremath{{}^{2}} initial scaled particle state, and independent standard Gaussian innovations at all coordinates and times, independent of initialization. Write \(\rho_0\) for the initial scaled law, \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\) for the Gibbs target of the lifted \(V_\eta\), and choose marginal maximizers \(\theta_U\) for \(g_1+g_2\) and \(\theta_\eta\) for \(V_\eta\). Their existence is established in the supporting modules.



Precisely, the scaled state, lifted potential, and normalized target are


\[z=(\theta,x^1/\sqrt N,\ldots,x^N/\sqrt N),\qquad
W_{N,\eta}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^N V_\eta(\theta,\sqrt N z_i),\]
\[\Pi_{N,\eta}(dz)=Z_{N,\eta}^{-1}e^{-W_{N,\eta}(z)}dz.\]


A marginal maximizer of a pair potential \(V\) maximizes \(\theta\mapsto\int e^{-V(\theta,x)}dx\).



For PIPGLA assume also \(m\le L_1\), take any proximal parameter \(\lambda>0\), and \(0<\gamma\le2/(m+L_1)\). First apply the smooth-gradient step with its averaged parameter drift and Gaussian innovation:


\[\begin{aligned}
\widetilde\theta_n&=\theta_n-\frac\gamma N\sum_i\nabla_\theta g_1(\theta_n,x_n^i)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\,\xi_n^0,\\
\widetilde x_n^i&=x_n^i-\gamma\nabla_x g_1(\theta_n,x_n^i)+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\xi_n^i.
\end{aligned}\]


Then set \((a_i,b_i)=P_\lambda(\widetilde\theta_n,\widetilde x_n^i)\), \(\theta_{n+1}=N^{-1}\sum_i a_i\), and \(x_{n+1}^i=b_i\). Let \(\theta_\gamma\) maximize the marginal for \(V_\gamma\). The actual iterates satisfy


\[\begin{aligned}
\bigl(\mathbb E\|\theta_n-\theta_U\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}
\le{}&\frac{K|\lambda-\gamma|}{m\gamma}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma K^2}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}\\
&+\sqrt{(1-m\gamma)^n W_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\gamma})^2+\frac\gamma N C_N}.
\end{aligned}\]


All constants are explicit. Put \(D=d_\theta+Nd_x\), \(a=Nm\), \(b=NL_1\), \(c=NK\),


\[G=\sqrt{2b^2D/a+2c^2}+c,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{2D},\]
\[C_N=\frac{4b^2}{a^2}\left(\frac{G^2}{a}+\sigma^2\right)+\frac2a(Gc+c^2).\]


The first term is a proved proximal-parameter comparison. The remaining transport estimate comes from a constructed synchronous coupling and actual-law recurrence. The constants differ from the paper’s printed bound.


{\small\color{gray}Supporting proof: Section~\ref{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingTheorem-lean}.\par}
\section{\texorpdfstring{MYPGD: finite-\allowbreak{}particle noise removal}{MYPGD: finite-particle noise removal}}


Write \(U=g_1+g_2\) on \(\mathbb R^{d_\theta+d_x}\). Assume \(g_1\) is differentiable, \(m\)-strongly convex for \(m>0\), and has \(L_1\)-Lipschitz gradient. Assume \(g_2\) is finite-valued, convex, bounded below, and \(K\)-Lipschitz. Define the actual proximal minimizer and Moreau envelope by


\[P_\eta(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p\left\{g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}\right\},\quad
g_2^\eta(x)=g_2(P_\eta(x))+\frac{\|P_\eta(x)-x\|^2}{2\eta},\]
\[V_\eta=g_1+g_2^\eta,\qquad L_\eta=L_1+\eta^{-1},\qquad\eta>0.\]


Take \(N\ge1\), \(d_\theta,d_x\ge0\), an L\ensuremath{{}^{2}} initial scaled particle state, and independent standard Gaussian innovations at all coordinates and times, independent of initialization. Write \(\rho_0\) for the initial scaled law, \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\) for the Gibbs target of the lifted \(V_\eta\), and choose marginal maximizers \(\theta_U\) for \(g_1+g_2\) and \(\theta_\eta\) for \(V_\eta\). Their existence is established in the supporting modules.



Precisely, the scaled state, lifted potential, and normalized target are


\[z=(\theta,x^1/\sqrt N,\ldots,x^N/\sqrt N),\qquad
W_{N,\eta}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^N V_\eta(\theta,\sqrt N z_i),\]
\[\Pi_{N,\eta}(dz)=Z_{N,\eta}^{-1}e^{-W_{N,\eta}(z)}dz.\]


A marginal maximizer of a pair potential \(V\) maximizes \(\theta\mapsto\int e^{-V(\theta,x)}dx\).



MYPGD uses the MYIPLA drift and erases its parameter Gaussian noise, retaining latent Gaussian noise:


\[z_{n+1}=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla W_{N,\eta}(z_n)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\,M\xi_n,\]


where \(M\) is the orthogonal projection onto latent coordinates. Assume \(m\le L_\eta\) explicitly and \(0<\gamma\le2/(m+L_\eta)\). For every \(n\in\mathbb N\), the actual finite-particle recursion satisfies


\[\begin{aligned}
\bigl(\mathbb E\|\theta_n^{\rm PGD}-\theta_U\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}
\le{}&\sqrt{\frac{2d_\theta}{Nm}}+\sqrt{\frac{\eta K^2}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}\\
&+(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\eta})\\
&+\frac{33}{20}\frac{L_\eta}{m}\sqrt{d_x+\frac{d_\theta}{N}}\sqrt\gamma.
\end{aligned}\]


The additional \(\sqrt{2d_\theta/(Nm)}\) is a proved conservative cost of removing parameter noise. This finite-particle replacement does not assert the paper’s stronger mean-field metric or constants.

\chapter{Mathematical argument}
\section{\texorpdfstring{Construct the proximal map and differentiate the envelope}{Construct the proximal map and differentiate the envelope}}



For \(\eta>0\), adding \(\|p-x\|^2/(2\eta)\) to the finite convex function \(g_2(p)\) gives a coercive, strictly convex objective. It therefore has a unique minimizer \(P_\eta(x)\). The optimality inequalities at two starting points, added together, give firm nonexpansiveness:


\[\|P_\eta(x)-P_\eta(y)\|^2\le
\langle P_\eta(x)-P_\eta(y),x-y\rangle.\]


Expanding the squared difference of the residuals shows that \(I-P_\eta\) is also nonexpansive. Comparing the envelope value at \(x+u\) using the minimizer at \(x\), and conversely, gives upper and lower first-order bounds with a quadratic remainder. Their common derivative is


\[\nabla g_2^\eta(x)=\frac{x-P_\eta(x)}\eta.\]


The residual estimate makes this gradient \(\eta^{-1}\)-Lipschitz. The envelope is convex, so \(V_\eta=g_1+g_2^\eta\) retains curvature \(m\) and has smoothness \(L_\eta=L_1+\eta^{-1}\). These facts permit the smoothed particle recursion to use the Gaussian ULA transport theorem (see \cite[Section~3.45.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}).


\section{\texorpdfstring{Control smoothing by a finite potential sandwich}{Control smoothing by a finite potential sandwich}}



The minimizer comparison \(p=x\) gives \(g_2^\eta(x)\le g_2(x)\). Lipschitzness supplies the reverse estimate: for every \(p\),


\[g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}
\ge g_2(x)-K\|p-x\|+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}
\ge g_2(x)-\frac{\eta K^2}{2}.\]


The last inequality completes the scalar square in \(\|p-x\|\). Evaluating at the actual minimizer proves


\[V_\eta\le U\le V_\eta+\delta_\eta,\qquad\delta_\eta=\eta K^2/2.\]


Exponentiation and latent integration preserve this finite sandwich. If \(F_U,F_\eta\) are the negative log marginal likelihoods, then \(F_\eta\le F_U\le F_\eta+\delta_\eta\). In particular the nonsmooth original marginal is integrable and its center exists by comparison with the strongly convex smoothed model.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Transfer the potential gap to the statistical centers}{Transfer the potential gap to the statistical centers}}



Let \(\theta_U\) and \(\theta_\eta\) minimize \(F_U\) and \(F_\eta\), respectively. The marginal strong-convexity theorem (see \cite[Section~3.12.3]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}) makes \(F_\eta\) \(m\)-strongly convex. Quadratic growth at its minimizer then gives the first inequality below. Combining it with the sandwich and minimality of \(\theta_U\) gives the full chain


\[\begin{aligned}
\frac m2\|\theta_U-\theta_\eta\|^2
&\le F_\eta(\theta_U)-F_\eta(\theta_\eta)\\
&\le F_U(\theta_U)-F_\eta(\theta_\eta)\\
&\le F_U(\theta_\eta)-F_\eta(\theta_\eta)
\le\delta_\eta.
\end{aligned}\]


Hence \(\|\theta_U-\theta_\eta\|\le K\sqrt{\eta/m}\). This is a finite estimate for actual marginal-likelihood centers, valid for every positive \(\eta\). It does not require differentiating the original nonsmooth potential. Its order is \(\sqrt\eta\), so a linear smoothing rate cannot be substituted into the final theorem.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Derive the MYIPLA bound around the original center}{Derive the MYIPLA bound around the original center}}



MYIPLA is exactly IPLA for \(V_\eta\). The lifted-potential results (see \cite[Section~3.1.3]{LangevinLean2026IPLA} and \cite[Section~3.1.4]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}) give curvature \(Nm\) and smoothness \(NL_\eta\); the state dimension is \(D=d_\theta+Nd_x\). The ULA step is \(\gamma/N\), so the contraction factor is \(1-m\gamma\). Apply the small-step Gaussian ULA theorem (see \cite[Section~3.45.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}) and then the projected RMS inequality (see \cite[Section~3.48.9]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}).




The sharp concentration result for the smoothed marginal (see Section~\ref{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpIPLA-lean-1}) gives radius \(\sqrt{d_\theta/(Nm)}\). Finally Minkowski's inequality adds the deterministic displacement between the smoothed and original centers. Thus the four terms are respectively


\[K\sqrt{\eta/m},\quad\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}},\quad
(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\eta}),\quad
\frac{33}{20}\frac{L_\eta}{m}\sqrt{\gamma\left(d_x+\frac{d_\theta}{N}\right)}.\]


Reducing \(\eta\) improves the first term but increases \(L_\eta\) and tightens the admissible step restriction. The smoothing and discretization parameters must therefore be chosen together. Each term has been controlled for the actual Gaussian particle law.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Control PIPGLA at the matching proximal parameter}{Control PIPGLA at the matching proximal parameter}}



First take \(\lambda=\gamma\). The smooth-gradient/\allowbreak{}noise step is followed by pairwise proximal maps, and the common parameter is their average. Use the same Gaussian increment for the numerical step and the Gibbs-stationary Langevin reference for \(V_\gamma\) supplied by \cite[Section~3.42.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}. Expanding the squared coupling error keeps the inner products generated by the proximal optimality inequality; these terms must be combined before any absolute-value estimate is made.




With \(a=Nm\), \(b=NL_1\), \(c=NK\), the stationary drift and noise bounds can be expressed as \(G=\sqrt{2b^2D/a+2c^2}+c\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{2D}\). The local increment bound is \(Gs+\sigma\sqrt s\). Squaring it uses \((u+v)^2\le2u^2+2v^2\), and \(s\le1/a\) absorbs its higher-order powers into the explicit constant


\[C_N=\frac{4b^2}{a^2}\left(\frac{G^2}{a}+\sigma^2\right)+\frac2a(Gc+c^2).\]


More explicitly, with \(\delta=N\gamma K^2/2=sc^2/2\), the one-step forcing is


\[\mathcal F=(1+(as)^{-1})[bs(Gs+\sigma\sqrt s)]^2+2s\delta+2s^2Gc+s^2c^2.\]


The first term is the weighted smooth drift defect. Convex support, stationarity and orthogonality to future Brownian increments combine the reference-force cross term with the original nonsmooth energy difference, leaving \(2s\delta+2s^2Gc\). The last term is the genuine proximal energy remainder. Since \(s\le1/a\), bounding the drift-plus-noise square gives \(\mathcal F\le as^2C_N\). The resulting squared-error recurrence has contraction \(1-a s=1-m\gamma\). Taking the initial coupling infimum and summing the geometric series gives


\[W_2(\rho_n,\Pi_{N,\gamma})^2
\le(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\gamma})^2+\frac\gamma N C_N.\]


After taking a square root, projection adds the sharp equilibrium radius, and the finite center comparison adds \(K\sqrt{\gamma/m}\). The squared recurrence explains why its transient factor occurs inside a square root in the theorem.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Compare different positive proximal parameters}{Compare different positive proximal parameters}}



For a convex \(K\)-Lipschitz penalty, the proximal optimality relations and monotonicity of its subgradient give


\[\|P_\lambda(x)-P_\eta(x)\|\le K|\lambda-\eta|.\]


Apply this comparison particle by particle, using the same initial state and innovations in the two splitting chains. Averaging the parameter outputs and using the scaled norm controls the full-state discrepancy by \(K|\lambda-\eta|\) per step. The sharp smooth-step theorem (see \cite[Section~3.10.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}) gives contraction \(q=1-m\gamma\); nonexpansiveness of the proximal map preserves that contraction.




Starting from zero discrepancy, the finite geometric sum therefore bounds the difference of the two chains by \(K|\lambda-\eta|/(1-q)=K|\lambda-\eta|/(m\gamma)\), uniformly in iteration count. Set \(\eta=\gamma\) and combine with the matching-parameter result. The triangle inequality for parameter RMS adds precisely the first term of the displayed PIPGLA theorem.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Compare MYPGD with the full-\allowbreak{}noise chain}{Compare MYPGD with the full-noise chain}}



Start from any finite-cost coupling of the current MYPGD and MYIPLA laws, and extend it by a fresh independent standard Gaussian vector. Use that vector's latent block in both updates and retain its parameter block only in the full-noise update. The omitted parameter innovation is centered and has squared \(L^2\) norm \(2\gamma d_\theta/N\). Independence therefore cancels its expected cross term with the deterministic forward discrepancy.




The sharp smooth-step contraction theorem (see \cite[Section~3.10.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}) makes the shared forward map contract by \(1-m\gamma\). Thus the constructed output coupling has squared cost at most \((1-m\gamma)^2\) times the input squared cost plus the omitted-noise variance. Taking the infimum over input couplings proves, for \(E_n=W_2(\rho_n^{\mathrm{PGD}},\rho_n^{\mathrm{MYIPLA}})^2\),


\[E_{n+1}\le(1-m\gamma)^2E_n+\frac{2\gamma d_\theta}{N},\qquad E_0=0.\]


The initial distance is zero because the two chains have the same initial law. Their finite moments justify all transport squares. The step restriction implies \(0\le m\gamma\le1\). Hence \(1-(1-m\gamma)^2=m\gamma(2-m\gamma)\ge m\gamma\), and geometric summation gives \(E_n\le2d_\theta/(Nm)\). The parameter projection is nonexpansive by \cite[Section~3.1.6]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}, so the projected RMS inequality from \cite[Section~3.48.9]{LangevinLean2026ULASC} and this transport bound add \(\sqrt{2d_\theta/(Nm)}\) to the MYIPLA parameter bound.




This establishes the stated finite-particle MYPGD guarantee directly. The added radius is conservative and remains present for finite \(N\); it does not imply a stronger mean-field metric estimate. The supporting mean-field construction and particle comparisons are developed separately.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Exact maps for MYIPLA, PIPULA and PIPGLA}{Exact maps for MYIPLA, PIPULA and PIPGLA}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean}


The three proximal particle schemes use the same scaled state geometry but place deterministic drift, proximity and Gaussian noise in different orders. These pointwise definitions are valid before choosing a probability law. Residual calculus then identifies which updates are exactly Euler steps for a Moreau-smoothed potential and which require a separate splitting analysis.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.4]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The pair map and coordinate outputs}{The pair map and coordinate outputs}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-1}


Define \(\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)_\theta=N^{-1}\sum_i(P(J_i z))_\theta\) and \(\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)_{x_i}=N^{-1/2}(P(J_i z))_x\). These coordinate formulas specify the map separately on its parameter and latent blocks. This averaging is part of the algorithm, and does not assert that the map minimizes a lifted objective.





The map acts on every physical pair using the same old parameter, then returns one parameter by averaging all proposed parameter outputs. Each proposed latent output returns only to its own particle block. The factors \(1/N\) and \(1/\sqrt N\) thus have different roles. They encode a common parameter and the scaled latent geometry, rather than independent parameter copies evolving alongside the particles.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Where each algorithm places its noise}{Where each algorithm places its noise}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-2}


PIPGLA, v3 equations (11)–(14), first takes the noisy smooth \(g_1\) step and then applies the averaged pairwise proximal map. In scaled coordinates it is


\[
y_n=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla\!\left(\sum_i g_1(J_i z_n)\right)
 +\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{N}}\xi_n,
\qquad z_{n+1}=\operatorname{Pair}_{P_\lambda}(y_n).
\]


PIPULA is the special MYIPLA update with \(g_1=0\) and \(\lambda=\gamma\), followed by Gaussian noise. Both distinctions are encoded in separate definitions and proved reduction identities. The PIPGLA proximity step occurs after the noise; its target in the v3 convergence analysis is the original nonsmooth Gibbs model. The MYIPLA theorem cannot be reused merely by renaming this composition.



For clarity, the expanded MYIPLA definition itself is

\[T_{\mathrm{MY}}(z,\xi)=(1-\gamma/\eta)z-\frac\gamma N\nabla F_{g_1}(z)+\frac\gamma\eta\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\xi.\]

These are exact definitions of pointwise maps. The residual-gradient theorem below proves when this expanded map equals a smoothed Euler step.





In MYIPLA, proximity is evaluated at the old state and appears through its residual in an explicit drift. In PIPGLA, proximity is evaluated after the smooth drift and Gaussian perturbation. These compositions give different random variables even with the same innovation. Therefore their deterministic identities must be checked separately before any convergence theorem is transferred from a smooth Euler chain.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.1.7]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{A recursion with supplied innovations}{A recursion with supplied innovations}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-3}


For an update \(T\), define \(Z_0=z_0\) and \(Z_{n+1}=T(Z_n,\xi_n)\). The zero and successor lemmas unfold these equations. Supplying the three explicit updates defines their full chains for any initial state and innovation sequence; Gaussianity, measurability and convergence are not premises of these pointwise definitions.





A finite recursion requires only an update function, an initial state and a sequence of innovation vectors. Induction determines every iterate uniquely. When measurability and independence are later imposed, this same deterministic map of the inputs defines the probability law. Separating the pointwise construction from these probabilistic requirements allows the exact algorithm identities to be reused on different coupling spaces.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The PIPULA specialization is an identity}{The PIPULA specialization is an identity}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-4}


PIPULA has parameter update \(N^{-1}\sum_i(P(J_i z))_\theta+a\xi_\theta\). In MYIPLA set \(g_1=0\) and \(\eta=\gamma\ne0\). The lifted gradient of the constant potential vanishes, \(1-\gamma/\gamma=0\), and \(\gamma/\gamma=1\), so the updates agree. Replacing the update in the common recursion proves equality at every iteration.





When the two positive scales are equal, the coefficient multiplying the old state in the expanded residual update is zero and the coefficient multiplying its pairwise proximal image is one. With no smooth potential, nothing remains before the added noise except that image. The equality holds for each input and innovation, so induction gives equality of all finite-time chains and subsequently of their laws.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Recover physical parameter and particle formulas}{Recover physical parameter and particle formulas}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-5}


Differentiate the lifted sum: its parameter block is \(\sum_i\nabla_\theta g_1(J_i z)\), while its scaled latent block is \(\sqrt N\nabla_x g_1(J_i z)\). Substitution gives the displayed MYIPLA coordinate equations. Multiplying a latent coordinate by \(\sqrt N\) changes its noise coefficient to \(\sqrt{2\gamma}\) and its smooth force coefficient to \(\gamma\), using \(\sqrt N\sqrt{2\gamma/N}=\sqrt{2\gamma}\).





The physical parameter equation retains the average smooth force and the average proposed proximal parameter. The physical latent equation retains its individual force and proximal latent output. Multiplying by \(\sqrt N\) removes the scaling only from the latent block, including its Gaussian coefficient. This conversion shows directly that the state-space definitions implement the intended particle equations with parameter variance reduced by particle count.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Residual proximity is exactly an Euler step}{Residual proximity is exactly an Euler step}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-6}


Given any map \(P\) on one joint parameter-particle pair, define its particle action by applying \(P\) separately to each \(J_i z\), averaging the resulting parameter coordinates, and dividing each resulting physical particle coordinate by \(\sqrt N\). The common parameter is not optimized independently for each particle and then kept as \(N\) separate state variables; the averaging is part of the algorithm.

\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)=z-\frac\lambda N\nabla\!\left(\sum_i g_2^\lambda(J_i z)\right).
\]


Substituting this into the expanded MYIPLA formula cancels the extra multiples of \(z\), leaving


\[
z_{n+1}=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla\mathcal V_\lambda(z_n)
 +\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{N}}\,\xi_n.
\]


The coordinate proofs also recover parameter noise \(\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\) and physical-particle noise \(\sqrt{2\gamma}\). The full recursion identity follows by induction on the iteration count.

Inserting this identity into MYIPLA cancels the extraneous old-state coefficients. \section{\texorpdfstring{Actual proximal minimizers and optimality}{Actual proximal minimizers and optimality}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean}


Proximity is defined by global minimization of a convex function plus a positive quadratic penalty. Its residual is characterized by a true supporting subgradient. Monotonicity of these supports gives uniqueness and firm nonexpansiveness, while finite-dimensional compactness and a lower bound supply existence. These facts justify every subsequent use of the actual proximity map.




On a real inner product space let \(Q_{\lambda,x}(p)=f(p)+\|p-x\|^2/(2\lambda)\). Convexity, a lower bound, finite dimension and \(\lambda>0\) construct a true minimizer; the variational lemmas also apply to any supplied minimizer.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Quadratic objective and global support}{Quadratic objective and global support}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-1}


The definitions mean \(p\in\arg\min Q_{\lambda,x}\) and \(s\in\partial f(p)\) precisely when \(f(p)+\langle s,y-p\rangle\le f(y)\) for every \(y\). Apply the support inequalities at \((p,s)\) and \((q,t)\) to each other, add and cancel function values to obtain \(\langle s-t,p-q\rangle\ge0\). No differentiability is involved.





The support condition is global: it compares the function at every competitor with an affine function touching at the support point. At two support points, use each point as the other's competitor. The function values cancel upon addition, leaving \(0\le\langle s-t,p-q\rangle\). This is monotonicity of the subdifferential, and it remains valid for finite convex functions with nondifferentiable corners.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The residual characterizes the minimum}{The residual characterizes the minimum}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-2}


A vector \(s\) is a subgradient of \(g_2\) at \(p\) when \(g_2(p)+\langle s,y-p\rangle\le g_2(y)\) for every \(y\). The exact proximal optimality condition is


\[
p=P_\lambda(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{x-p}{\lambda}\in\partial g_2(p).
\]


For necessity, compare the minimizing objective at \(p\) with its value along \(p+t(y-p)\), use convexity, and let positive \(t\) become arbitrarily small. For sufficiency, insert the supporting-hyperplane inequality into the difference of the two quadratic objectives. This proves the global minimum directly, without assuming differentiability of \(g_2\).



Apply monotonicity of subgradients to the residuals at two inputs. It gives firm nonexpansiveness,


\[
\|P_\lambda(x)-P_\lambda(y)\|^2
\le\langle x-y,P_\lambda(x)-P_\lambda(y)\rangle.
\]


Cauchy–Schwarz gives nonexpansiveness of \(P_\lambda\). Expanding the square gives nonexpansiveness of \(I-P_\lambda\) as well. Those are derived geometric facts, not premises stored in a proximal-map structure.




To see the necessity calculation explicitly, fix \(y\), put \(d=y-p\), and compare the proximal objective at \(p\) with the competitor \(p+td\), \(0<t\le1\). Convexity bounds \(f(p+td)\) by \((1-t)f(p)+tf(y)\). After expanding the square and dividing by positive \(t\), \[f(y)-f(p)\ge\left\langle\frac{x-p}{\lambda},d\right\rangle-\frac{t}{2\lambda}\|d\|^2.\] Letting \(t\downarrow0\) gives the support inequality. Thus the variational argument establishes a subgradient at the chosen minimizer without assuming differentiability.





For sufficiency, expanding the quadratic difference gives \(Q_{\lambda,x}(y)-Q_{\lambda,x}(p)\ge\|y-p\|^2/(2\lambda)\) when the residual is a support at \(p\). Thus the support not only proves global optimality but quantifies the objective increase away from \(p\). Necessity is obtained by short line-segment competitors and a limit, so the equivalence does not assume a differential first-order condition for the original nonsmooth function.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Firmness gives uniqueness and two contractions}{Firmness gives uniqueness and two contractions}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-3}


Apply subgradient monotonicity to \((x-p)/\lambda\) and \((y-q)/\lambda\). Multiplying by \(\lambda\) gives \(\|p-q\|^2\le\langle x-y,p-q\rangle\). For a common input, the right side is zero, so \(p=q\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\|p-q\|\le\|x-y\|\). Finally expand \(\|(x-y)-(p-q)\|^2\); firmness bounds its mixed term and proves \(\|(x-p)-(y-q)\|\le\|x-y\|\).





Put \(u=x-y\) and \(v=p-q\). Firmness gives \(\langle u,v\rangle\ge\|v\|^2\), hence \(\|u-v\|^2\le\|u\|^2-\|v\|^2\le\|u\|^2\). This proves contraction of the residual map \(I-P_\lambda\) with the same unit constant. If the inputs coincide, firmness forces \(v=0\), which proves uniqueness independently of the compact-attainment argument.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Compact attainment and the chosen map}{Compact attainment and the chosen map}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-4}


For \(\lambda>0\), define


\[
Q_{\lambda,x}(p)=g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\lambda},\qquad
P_\lambda(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p Q_{\lambda,x}(p).
\]


A finite-valued convex function on a finite-dimensional real space is continuous. If \(g_2\ge b\), then outside a sufficiently large ball the quadratic term makes \(Q_{\lambda,x}(p)\) exceed \(Q_{\lambda,x}(x)=g_2(x)\). A continuous function attains its minimum on that closed ball, and the same point therefore minimizes it globally. The proof explicitly constructs a suitable radius from \(\sqrt{2\lambda(g_2(x)-b)}+1\).






Choose a closed ball centered at \(x\) that contains every point where the objective can improve on its value at \(x\). The lower bound on the potential and positivity of the quadratic coefficient provide such a ball. Continuity gives a minimizer on this compact set; outside it the objective is larger than the known competitor value. The minimizing point is therefore global and is the unique point characterized by the residual support condition.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Moreau envelope: gradient, smoothness and finite bias}{Moreau envelope: gradient, smoothness and finite bias}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean}


The Moreau envelope is an actual minimum value, not a formal smoothing symbol. Comparing neighboring minimizing objectives proves a two-sided quadratic remainder and identifies its gradient with the scaled proximal residual. A global value Lipschitz bound on the original potential also yields finite residual and uniform value-gap estimates, which later control smoothing bias.




Let \(f\) be finite-valued, convex and bounded below on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, and \(\lambda>0\). Set \(P=P_\lambda\), \(f^\lambda(x)=Q_{\lambda,x}(P x)\), and \(r(x)=(x-Px)/\lambda\).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Value bounds and the exact residual}{Value bounds and the exact residual}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-1}


The envelope is the objective value at the actual chosen minimizer. Testing its objective with \(p=x\) proves \(f^\lambda(x)\le f(x)\). If \(f\ge b\), the nonnegative quadratic penalty also proves \(f^\lambda\ge b\). The residual definition is at this point only a vector formula; its gradient interpretation is proved below.





Testing the minimization problem with the input itself removes the quadratic penalty and gives the upper value bound. At the minimizing point, the original lower bound and nonnegative penalty give the lower value bound. These comparisons show that the envelope is finite everywhere on the positive-parameter domain. They do not yet identify its derivative; that identification requires the neighboring-objective comparison.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Global supports and quadratic upper remainder}{Global supports and quadratic upper remainder}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-2}


Use the residual subgradient at \(P x\), test it at \(P y\), and expand the two quadratic penalties. The remaining squared norm is nonnegative, yielding \(f^\lambda(x)+\langle r(x),y-x\rangle\le f^\lambda(y)\). Conversely use \(P x\) as a competitor in the objective at \(y\), giving an upper remainder \(\|y-x\|^2/(2\lambda)\). For \(z=ax+by\), multiply the supports at \(z\) by \(a,b\ge0\), \(a+b=1\); their linear terms cancel and prove convexity.





For the lower estimate, the residual support at \(P_\lambda x\) controls the change in original potential between the two minimizers. Combining it with their quadratic penalties leaves a nonnegative square. For the upper estimate, keep the old minimizer as a competitor at the new input and expand only its quadratic penalty. The resulting remainder is bounded above by \(\|y-x\|^2/(2\lambda)\), uniformly in the choice of nearby input.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Nonexpansiveness and the derivative}{Nonexpansiveness and the derivative}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-3}


Define \(g_2^\lambda(x)=Q_{\lambda,x}(P_\lambda(x))\) and \(r_\lambda(x)=\lambda^{-1}(x-P_\lambda(x))\). The subgradient inequality at the actual minimizer proves the lower support bound, while comparison with that same minimizer in the neighboring objective proves the upper bound:


\[
0\le g_2^\lambda(y)-g_2^\lambda(x)
 -\langle r_\lambda(x),y-x\rangle
\le\frac{\|y-x\|^2}{2\lambda}.
\]


The right side is little-o of \(\|y-x\|\), so the envelope is differentiable and \(\nabla g_2^\lambda=r_\lambda\). The global supports imply convexity. Nonexpansiveness of \(I-P_\lambda\) then yields


\[
\|\nabla g_2^\lambda(x)-\nabla g_2^\lambda(y)\|
\le\lambda^{-1}\|x-y\|.
\]


The gradient is continuous, so the actual envelope is continuously differentiable. This establishes the regularity needed by the existing Langevin theorem even when the original potential has corners.






With \(y=x+h\), the remainder divided by \(\|h\|\) is at most \(\|h\|/(2\lambda)\), which tends to zero. This is the Fréchet differentiability criterion with derivative \(h\mapsto\langle r_\lambda(x),h\rangle\). The residual-map contraction yields its global gradient Lipschitz constant \(1/\lambda\). Thus the envelope has the exact regularity required for a smooth Langevin step, even though the original function need not be differentiable.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Bounded residual and a sharp uniform gap}{Bounded residual and a sharp uniform gap}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-4}


Assume \(f\) is convex, bounded below and globally \(K\)-Lipschitz, with \(K\ge0\) and \(\lambda>0\). Fix an input \(x\), let \(p=P_\lambda(x)\) be its actual proximal minimizer, and write \(r=(x-p)/\lambda\). Proximal optimality gives \(r\in\partial f(p)\). Test this support at \(p+r\), then apply value Lipschitzness:


\[
\|r\|^2\le f(p+r)-f(p)\le K\|r\|.
\]


If \(r=0\), the desired bound is immediate; otherwise cancel the positive norm. Thus \(\|r\|\le K\), and the identity \(x-p=\lambda r\) gives the displacement estimate \(\|x-p\|\le\lambda K\).




For the envelope value, the competitor \(p=x\) gives \(f^\lambda(x)\le f(x)\). To obtain the reverse comparison, put \(d=\|p-x\|\) and use the exact minimizing objective value:


\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x)-f^\lambda(x)
&=f(x)-f(p)-\frac{d^2}{2\lambda}\\
&\le Kd-\frac{d^2}{2\lambda}\\
&=\frac{\lambda K^2}{2}-\frac{(d-\lambda K)^2}{2\lambda}
\le\frac{\lambda K^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\]


The completed-square remainder is nonnegative because \(\lambda>0\). Combining both comparisons yields the uniform finite gap \(0\le f(x)-f^\lambda(x)\le\lambda K^2/2\) for every input. For \(U=g_1+f\) and \(V_\lambda=g_1+f^\lambda\), the same gap gives \(V_\lambda\le U\le V_\lambda+\lambda K^2/2\). This pointwise sandwich can be exponentiated and integrated to compare the actual marginal potentials; it requires no Taylor expansion or passage of an asymptotic remainder through an integral.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Quadratic growth needs only strong convexity}{Quadratic growth needs only strong convexity}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Perturbation-lean-1}


Let \(F\) be \(m\)-strongly convex with \(m\ge0\), and let \(z\) be a global minimizer. Fix any comparator \(x\), and set


\[
A=\frac m2\|x-z\|^2,\qquad G=F(x)-F(z).
\]


Both numbers are nonnegative. For \(0<t<1\), apply strong convexity along the segment from \(z\) to \(x\), and use global minimality at the intermediate point:


\[
F(z)\le F((1-t)z+tx)
\le (1-t)F(z)+tF(x)-t(1-t)A.
\]


Subtract \(F(z)\) and divide by the positive number \(t\). This gives \((1-t)A\le G\) for every such \(t\). To conclude using only a finite comparison, suppose \(G<A\). Then \(A>0\), and the choice


\[
t=\frac{A-G}{2A}\in(0,1/2]
\]


is admissible. But it makes \((1-t)A=(A+G)/2>G\), contradicting the preceding inequality. Therefore \(A\le G\), which is the sharp growth bound


\[
F(x)\ge F(z)+\frac m2\|x-z\|^2.
\]


No derivative of \(F\) is used: the argument needs only the convex-combination inequality and actual global minimality. In the marginal-center comparison, this lower bound turns a finite increase in marginal potential into an upper bound on the squared distance between centers.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Integrate the gap before taking logarithms}{Integrate the gap before taking logarithms}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Perturbation-lean-2}

\[
e^{-\delta}k_V(\theta)\le k_U(\theta)\le k_V(\theta),
\qquad k_W(\theta)=\int e^{-W(\theta,x)}dx.
\]


The smooth strongly convex comparison potential \(V\) supplies positive finite integrals and an integrable Gaussian bound. The original marginal is continuous and decays at infinity; hence it has an actual maximizer. Taking negative logarithms, which is justified by positivity, gives


\[
F_V(\theta)\le F_U(\theta)\le F_V(\theta)+\delta,
\qquad F_W=-\log k_W.
\]


Let \(\theta_U\) minimize \(F_U\) and \(\theta_V\) minimize the \(m\)-strongly convex \(F_V\). Minimality and the sandwich imply \(F_V(\theta_U)-F_V(\theta_V)\le\delta\). Strong-convexity growth about its minimizer therefore yields


\[
\frac m2\|\theta_U-\theta_V\|^2\le\delta.
\]


This argument never differentiates the original marginal and never integrates an asymptotic remainder.



The sharp quadratic-growth lemma uses strong convexity and global minimality, without differentiability of the marginal potential. The comparison model supplies an integrable Gaussian bound for the original weights, giving continuity and existence of an original marginal maximizer by dominated convergence and decay at infinity. Finite positive marginal integrals are established before logarithms and real conversions.




For \(V\le U\le V+\delta\), exponentiation reverses the potential order: \(e^{-\delta}e^{-V}\le e^{-U}\le e^{-V}\). Integrating each latent fiber gives \(e^{-\delta}M_V(\theta)\le M_U(\theta)\le M_V(\theta)\). Positivity and finiteness of the reference marginal then imply the same for \(M_U\); only after that can one use monotonicity of the logarithm to obtain \(F_V\le F_U\le F_V+\delta\), where \(F=-\log M\). These guards exclude taking logarithms of zero or infinite marginal integrals.





Exponentiation reverses the potential order, while integration preserves the order of nonnegative functions. Because the reference marginal is positive and finite, the original marginal inherits both properties. Taking logarithms is now legitimate and gives the same finite additive gap \(\delta\). Performing integration before logarithms avoids any unsupported replacement of a logarithm of an integral by an integral of a logarithm.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Sharp Gibbs concentration by dilation}{Sharp Gibbs concentration by dilation}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean}


A radial contraction of space compares an unnormalized Gibbs partition with its second moment. A one-sided scalar derivative at the identity dilation yields the sharp dimension-over-curvature constant. The argument differentiates an explicit polynomial in the dilation factor and requires only continuity and strong convexity of the potential itself.




Let \(F\) be continuous and \(q\)-strongly convex on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of dimension \(d\), with \(q>0\) and global minimizer \(z\). Gaussian domination supplies the integrals used below.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{A radial contraction compares partition integrals}{A radial contraction compares partition integrals}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean-1}


Strong convexity and minimality imply \(F(z+r(x-z))\le F(x)-\frac q2(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2\) for \(0<r<1\). Exponentiate and use \(e^u\ge1+u\). Integrating and changing variables under \(x\mapsto z+r(x-z)\), whose Jacobian is \(r^d\), yields

\[Z+\frac q2(1-r^2)M\le r^{-d}Z,\qquad Z=\int e^{-F},\quad M=\int\|x-z\|^2e^{-F}.\]


Let \(w(x)=e^{-F(x)}\), \(Z=\int w\), \(M=\int\|x-z\|^2w(x)\,dx\), and \(d=\dim H\). Continuity and positive strong convexity first give integrability of both \(w\) and \(\|x-z\|^2w\). Strong convexity and the minimizer growth bound combine to give \[F(z+r(x-z))\le F(x)-\tfrac q2(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2.\] Exponentiation and \(e^u\ge1+u\) imply \[w(z+r(x-z))\ge w(x)+\tfrac q2(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2w(x).\]





Strong convexity along the segment from the mode to \(x\), together with quadratic growth from that mode, gives the radial deficit \(q(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2/2\). Exponentiating and using \(e^u\ge1+u\) converts this into a density-plus-moment lower bound. Dilation changes volume by \(r^d\), so integrating the dilated density contributes \(r^{-d}Z\). Gaussian domination makes both sides finite.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Take the sharp limit and normalize}{Take the sharp limit and normalize}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean-2}


Multiply the dilation inequality by \(r^d\) and define \(\Phi(r)=r^d[Z+(q/2)(1-r^2)M]\). Then \(\Phi(r)\le Z=\Phi(1)\) for \(0<r<1\). Its left difference quotients at one are nonnegative. Differentiating only this scalar polynomial expression gives \(\Phi'(1)=dZ-qM\ge0\), so \(M\le dZ/q\). The partition function \(Z\) is finite and strictly positive, as proved by strong-convexity Gaussian domination. The actual Gibbs normalization gives

\[\int\|x-z\|^2\pi(dx)=M/Z\le d/q,\qquad\pi(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{-F(x)}dx.\]

The proof differentiates no potential and remains valid for continuous nonsmooth strongly convex \(F\).






After obtaining \(qM\le dZ\), strict positivity of \(Z\) permits division. The normalized Gibbs moment is exactly \(M/Z\), by its density definition and the finite integrability of the moment weight. Therefore the estimate is \(d/q\). Neither a derivative of the potential nor a boundary integration identity is needed, making the estimate directly applicable to continuous nonsmooth strongly convex energies.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The one-\allowbreak{}sided tangent calculation gives d/\allowbreak{}q}{The one-sided tangent calculation gives d/q}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean-3}


Retain \(Z,M,d\) from the dilation inequality and define \[\Phi(r)=r^d\bigl[Z+\tfrac q2(1-r^2)M\bigr].\] For \(0<r<1\), dilation proves \(\Phi(r)\le Z=\Phi(1)\). Thus for \(-1<u<0\), the quotient \((\Phi(1+u)-\Phi(1))/u\) is nonnegative: both numerator and denominator are nonpositive.

Its limit equals \(dZ-qM\), computed by the product rule on the explicit scalar expression. The limit is therefore nonnegative. \subsection{\texorpdfstring{Concentration of the actual marginal target}{Concentration of the actual marginal target}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpIPLA-lean-1}


For a continuous \(a\)-strongly convex potential \(f\) with minimizer \(x_\star\), the new dilation argument proves

\section{\texorpdfstring{The nonsmooth single-\allowbreak{}pair energy estimate}{The nonsmooth single-pair energy estimate}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-ProximalEnergy-lean}


The energy inequality compares the true proximal output with any comparator while retaining the original nonsmooth potential difference at the input. An input subgradient controls the difference between input and proximal output. Completing a square produces a forcing term proportional to the square of the proximal scale, the correct quantity for summing particle energy estimates.




For convex \(f\), a true proximal minimizer \(p=P_\eta(x)\), positive \(\eta\), and a genuine input subgradient \(s\in\partial f(x)\), proximity controls both squared distance and the original nonsmooth energy.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Expand supports and complete a square}{Expand supports and complete a square}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ProximalEnergy-lean-1}


Let \(p=P_\lambda(y)\), \(s\in\partial g_2(y)\), and \(a\) be any comparator. Proximal optimality supplies the residual subgradient at \(p\). Combine its support inequality at \(a\) with the support inequality for \(s\) at \(p\), expand the squared distances, and use the nonnegative square \(\|(y-p)-\lambda s\|^2\). The result is v3 Lemma A.8:


\[
\|p-a\|^2\le\|y-a\|^2
 -2\lambda\bigl(g_2(y)-g_2(a)\bigr)+\lambda^2\|s\|^2.
\]


If \(\|s\|^2\le C\), replace the last term by \(\lambda^2C\). The proof works with any genuine subgradient satisfying that bound; it does not assume an energy estimate or a convergence statement. Taking the minimal section in C1 is one way to supply such a vector.





Write \(d=x-p\). The residual support at \(p\) implies \(2\eta[f(p)-f(a)]\le2\langle d,p-a\rangle\). The input support gives \(f(x)-f(p)\le\langle s,d\rangle\). Add these to the squared-distance expansion. The remaining scalar expression is \(2\eta\langle s,d\rangle-\|d\|^2\le\eta^2\|s\|^2\), obtained from \(\|d-\eta s\|^2\ge0\).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Instantiate the chosen point and bound the forcing}{Instantiate the chosen point and bound the forcing}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ProximalEnergy-lean-2}


The existence theorem proves that the chosen \(P_\eta(x)\) meets the variational premise. Substitution gives \(\|P_\eta(x)-a\|^2\le\|x-a\|^2-2\eta(f(x)-f(a))+\eta^2\|s\|^2\). If \(\|s\|^2\le C\), multiply by the nonnegative \(\eta^2\) to replace the final term by \(\eta^2 C\). The vector must satisfy the true support inequality; a merely named subgradient is insufficient.




Expand \(\|p-y\|^2=\|x-y\|^2-2\langle x-p,x-y\rangle+\|x-p\|^2\), and use the residual subgradient at \(p\) to replace its pairing with the potential difference. A supplied subgradient \(s_x\in\partial f(x)\) bounds the remaining difference \(f(x)-f(p)\) by \(\langle s_x,x-p\rangle\). Completing the square gives \(2\lambda\langle s_x,x-p\rangle-\|x-p\|^2\le\lambda^2\|s_x\|^2\). Thus a bound \(\|s_x\|^2\le C\) produces the forcing \(\lambda^2C\). This algebra is why that forcing has a squared proximal scale.





The comparator is arbitrary, so the same deterministic inequality can later be applied with a random reference point pointwise. A bound on \(\|s\|^2\) supplies only the last forcing term; it does not replace the requirement that \(s\) be a genuine supporting subgradient. The positive scale ensures both actual minimizer existence and validity of the residual optimality characterization used in the argument.

\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-ParticleEnergy-lean}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Averaging squares in the shared parameter}{Averaging squares in the shared parameter}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ParticleEnergy-lean-1}


Cauchy–Schwarz for finite sums gives \(N(N^{-1}\sum_i b_i)^2\le\sum_i b_i^2\). Apply it separately to each parameter coordinate of the pairwise outputs. Each latent block instead satisfies an exact equality after multiplying by \(N\), since \((\sqrt N)^2=N\). Summing all coordinates yields

\[\|\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)-a\|^2\le\frac1N\sum_i\|P(J_i z)-J_i a\|^2.\]
\[N\left|\frac1N\sum_i p_{i,\theta,j}-a_{\theta,j}\right|^2\le\sum_i|p_{i,\theta,j}-a_{\theta,j}|^2.\]

For particle coordinate \((i,j)\), unscaling instead gives the exact identity

\[N|N^{-1/2}p_{i,x,j}-a_{i,j}|^2=|p_{i,x,j}-\sqrt N a_{i,j}|^2.\]



For vectors \(b_i\), Jensen says \(\|N^{-1}\sum_i b_i\|^2\le N^{-1}\sum_i\|b_i\|^2\). Here \(b_i\) is the parameter component of the \(i\)-th output minus the common comparator parameter. Each latent component instead contributes exactly \(N^{-1}\|p_{i,x}-\sqrt N a_i\|^2\). Adding both blocks gives the displayed inequality for completely arbitrary pair maps, independently of their convex or proximal properties.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Sum genuine proximal energy inequalities}{Sum genuine proximal energy inequalities}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ParticleEnergy-lean-2}


The roadmap on v3 page 19 identifies averaged pairwise proximity with the proximal map of the lifted averaged potential. Shared parameter coordinates prevent that identity in general. The needed energy conclusion can nevertheless be proved without changing the algorithm.



For arbitrary pair outputs, Jensen's inequality for their averaged parameter gives


\[
\|\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)-a\|^2
\le\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\|P(J_i z)-J_i a\|^2.
\]


The scaled latent-coordinate contribution is an exact identity; only the parameter contribution requires an inequality. Apply the single-pair proximal energy result to every summand. Using \(\sum_i\|J_i(z-a)\|^2=N\|z-a\|^2\) and \(G_2=N^{-1}\sum_i g_2\circ J_i\) gives


\[
\|\operatorname{Pair}_{P_\lambda}(z)-a\|^2
\le\|z-a\|^2-2\lambda\bigl(G_2(z)-G_2(a)\bigr)+\lambda^2C.
\]

Because the two observables are Lipschitz, composition with a square-integrable state gives integrable potential values. Integral monotonicity may therefore be combined with ordinary linearity. This elementary bound is applied to actual finite expected energies, with no differentiation of the original nonsmooth potential.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Combine convex support, stationarity and the future estimate}{Combine convex support, stationarity and the future estimate}}

Minimizing each pair separately allows two different parameter choices; averaging them afterwards need not solve the single shared-parameter minimization problem. The explicit absolute-value objectives make each pair minimizer and the failed shared optimality test computable. This is why the main splitting proof uses summed pairwise energy and Jensen, rather than treating its averaged map as a lifted proximal minimizer.





Each pair minimization is allowed its own proposed parameter, while the lifted objective has one shared parameter. Averaging the proposed parameters need not minimize that shared objective. In the displayed absolute-value example the pairwise output has objective \(51/32\), whereas a feasible shared comparator has value \(3/2=48/32\). This strict inequality disproves the minimizer identity and motivates the valid Jensen-and-energy route.

\section{\texorpdfstring{A conditional consistency test for global assumptions}{A conditional consistency test for global assumptions}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-PIPULAConsistency-lean}


Positive strong monotonicity forces an unbounded field on a nontrivial Euclidean space. A globally Lipschitz potential has a bounded Moreau gradient, so combining that property with a positive global residual deficit can be inconsistent. The conclusion applies to the literal conjunction of those global assumptions and does not invalidate different full-potential hypotheses.




On a nontrivial real Hilbert space, a globally bounded field cannot be strongly monotone with a positive constant. This is used only for the literal specialization that also makes the full potential globally Lipschitz.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Boundedness contradicts positive global monotonicity}{Boundedness contradicts positive global monotonicity}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-PIPULAConsistency-lean-1}


Assume \(\|R(x)\|\le K\) and \(c\|x-y\|^2\le\langle x-y,R(x)-R(y)\rangle\) with \(c>0\). Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality give \(c\|x-y\|^2\le2K\|x-y\|\). Choose a nonzero vector and scale its norm beyond \(2K/c\). The two inequalities are incompatible.




If \(\|b(x)\|\le K\) globally but \(\langle b(x)-b(y),x-y\rangle\ge q\|x-y\|^2\) with \(q>0\), Cauchy–Schwarz implies \(q\|x-y\|\le2K\) for every distinct pair. Along a nonzero direction one can choose arbitrarily distant points, contradicting this uniform upper bound. The second theorem applies the obstruction to a true proximal residual: a globally Lipschitz potential gives a bounded residual. Thus the stated positive global deficit condition cannot be combined with those hypotheses in positive dimension.





Choose points separated by a distance \(r>2K/c\). Strong monotonicity gives a lower bound \(cr^2\) for the pairing, whereas boundedness gives an upper bound \(2Kr\). Division by positive \(r\) yields \(cr\le2K\), contradicting the choice. A nonzero direction supplies arbitrarily large \(r\), so positive dimension is essential to the obstruction.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Apply the obstruction to the true proximal residual}{Apply the obstruction to the true proximal residual}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-PIPULAConsistency-lean-2}


This check is conditional on how the appendix’s reduction is read. If setting \(g_1=0\) also carries A3 onto \(U=g_2\), then \(U\) is globally \(K\)-Lipschitz. Its actual Moreau gradient \(R_\gamma=\gamma^{-1}(I-P_\gamma)\) satisfies \(\|R_\gamma(x)\|\le K\). But B1 with positive deficit gives


\[
\frac{\mu}{\gamma}\|x-y\|^2
 \le\langle x-y,R_\gamma(x)-R_\gamma(y)\rangle
 \le2K\|x-y\|.
\]


A nontrivial real Hilbert space contains points at arbitrarily large distance, so these global premises are incompatible. This is kernel-checked for the actual proximal minimizer. If A3 is instead retained for an independent original decomposition \(U=g_1+g_2\), it does not make the full \(U\) Lipschitz; that reading requires its own full-envelope analysis. The result here is therefore \textbf{a conditional consistency check, not a blanket assertion that Corollary A.6 is false}. The separate PIPULA endpoint assumes strong convexity of the full potential and does not impose global Lipschitzness on it.





The actual residual \(R_\gamma=(I-P_\gamma)/\gamma\) is bounded by the potential's global value Lipschitz constant. The proximal deficit gives strong monotonicity with coefficient \(\mu/\gamma\), including its inverse-step factor. Substitution in the general obstruction proves incompatibility when \(\mu>0\) and the space is nontrivial. A decomposition whose nonsmooth component is Lipschitz need not make the entire potential Lipschitz, so its analysis is a different case.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Conditional scalar splitting recurrence and its strict endpoint}{Conditional scalar splitting recurrence and its strict endpoint}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Recurrence-lean}


These scalar results describe exactly what follows from a supplied affine recurrence with the displayed coefficients. They separate its strict geometric regime from the endpoint with unit multiplier. Their role is numerical interpretation; an actual stochastic one-step estimate must be proved independently before these formulas describe an algorithm.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.15]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Correct coefficients and their signs}{Correct coefficients and their signs}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Recurrence-lean-1}


Set

\[q=\frac{\lambda(1-\mu\gamma)}\gamma,\qquad c=\lambda\left(\frac{2\gamma LD}{N}+\lambda C\right).\]

Under the displayed nonnegative scalar premises, \(q,c\ge0\). When \(\gamma>0\) and \(\mu\gamma<1\), multiplying by positive denominators gives \(q<1\iff\lambda<\gamma/(1-\mu\gamma)\). The endpoint must be excluded from a geometric error floor. The subgradient forcing is \(\lambda^2C\), without an additional division by \(N\).




Write \(q=\lambda(1-\mu\gamma)/\gamma\) and \(c=2\lambda\gamma LD/N+\lambda^2C\). Positivity of \(\gamma\) and \(1-\mu\gamma\) permits multiplication through the inequality \(q<1\), giving precisely \(\lambda<\gamma/(1-\mu\gamma)\). The strict inequality is essential for dividing by \(1-q\); the nonstrict upper endpoint has zero denominator. Nonnegativity of \(q,c\) is established separately from the stated scalar sign hypotheses.





The recurrence coefficient contains the ratio of proximal to forward scales. Under positive denominators, \(q<1\) is equivalent to a strict upper bound on the proximal scale. Equality makes \(q=1\) and removes the geometric denominator. The forcing splits into a Gaussian contribution with \(1/N\) and a subgradient contribution without that factor, so their particle scaling must remain separate.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Sum a supplied affine recurrence}{Sum a supplied affine recurrence}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Recurrence-lean-2}


Assume \(e_{n+1}\le q e_n+c\). For \(q\ge0\), induction gives \(e_n\le q^ne_0+c\sum_{j<n}q^j\). If \(q<1\), the finite geometric identity gives \(e_n\le q^ne_0+c(1-q^n)/(1-q)\). If also \(c\ge0\), drop the nonpositive subtraction \(-cq^n/(1-q)\) to get \(e_n\le q^ne_0+c/(1-q)\). All three conclusions retain their recurrence hypothesis.






Induct on \(n\), multiplying the previous bound by nonnegative \(q\) before adding \(c\). This gives the exact finite sum of powers. If \(q<1\), use the geometric identity and its positive denominator. If \(c\ge0\), discarding the negative tail gives the simpler floor. Each conclusion retains the input recurrence as an explicit premise, rather than deriving it from an update.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.15.1]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.15.2]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.15.3]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Treat q = 1 and equal steps honestly}{Treat q = 1 and equal steps honestly}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Recurrence-lean-3}


At \(q=1\), induction gives the finite-horizon bound \(e_n\le e_0+nc\); there is no finite geometric floor. When \(\lambda=\gamma\) and the named denominators are nonzero, direct field algebra gives

\[\frac c{1-q}=\frac\gamma\mu\left(\frac{2LD}{N}+C\right).\]

This identity preserves the unscaled \(C\)-term and does not assert that an actual stochastic error satisfies the input recurrence.






At unit multiplier, the finite geometric sum is exactly \(n\); the valid estimate is linear growth, \(e_n\le e_0+nc\). Under equal positive scales and strict contraction, cancel the nonzero factors in \(c/(1-q)\). This leaves a Gaussian term divided by particle count and an unscaled subgradient term. The endpoint cannot be recovered by inserting zero into a geometric denominator.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Finite smoothing comparison for actual mean-\allowbreak{}field equations}{Finite smoothing comparison for actual mean-field equations}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean}


With the additional smoothness of the original nonsmooth-designated component, proximal optimality identifies the envelope score with the original score evaluated at the proximal point. A finite displacement bound then gives a uniform linear-in-scale score defect. Constructing both integral systems with common input lets that input cancel, and Grönwall controls their uniform timewise square-integrable difference.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.43]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{At differentiability points subgradients equal the gradient}{At differentiability points subgradients equal the gradient}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-1}


A supporting vector \(s\) makes \(x\mapsto f(x)-\langle s,x\rangle\) globally minimized at the support point \(p\). Its derivative there is zero, so \(s=\nabla f(p)\). Apply this to the genuine proximal residual to obtain the exact identity \(\nabla f^\eta(x)=\nabla f(P_\eta(x))\).





If \(s\) supports a differentiable convex function at \(p\), the function \(f(x)-\langle s,x\rangle\) has a global minimum at \(p\). Its derivative there vanishes, so \(s=\nabla f(p)\). Apply this to the actual proximal residual and the established envelope gradient formula. This proves an exact identity at every input, rather than an approximation to the score.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Smoothness gives a finite score defect}{Smoothness gives a finite score defect}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-2}


Compose \(\nabla f\), which is \(L_2\)-Lipschitz, with the nonexpansive map \(P_\eta\). The envelope gradient therefore has constant \(L_2\), independent of \(\eta\). The actual proximal displacement bound \(\|x-P_\eta(x)\|\le\eta K\) gives

\[\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f^\eta(x)\|\le L_2\|x-P_\eta(x)\|\le\eta L_2K.\]

This is a finite inequality, without a Taylor remainder.




When the original convex function is differentiable, a subgradient at a point equals its gradient; evaluate support along both signed directions and take the directional derivative. Proximal optimality therefore gives \(\nabla f^\eta(x)=\nabla f(P_\eta x)\). If \(\nabla f\) is \(L_2\)-Lipschitz and \(f\) is \(K\)-Lipschitz, then \[\|\nabla f^\eta(x)-\nabla f(x)\|\le L_2\|P_\eta x-x\|\le\eta L_2K.\] This finite score defect is the numerical forcing in the smoothing comparison.





The gradient of the envelope is \(\nabla f\circ P_\eta\). Composition with a nonexpansive map preserves the original score constant \(L_2\), even when the generic envelope bound \(1/\eta\) is much larger. Comparing with \(\nabla f(x)\) costs at most \(L_2\|P_\eta x-x\|\le\eta L_2K\). This uniform finite defect is the forcing used in the process comparison.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Recover component regularity from the regular sum}{Recover component regularity from the regular sum}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-3}


If \(U=f+g\) and \(f\) are differentiable, then \(g=U-f\) is differentiable. Gradient linearity gives \(\nabla g=\nabla U-\nabla f\), so gradient constants add: \(\operatorname{Lip}(\nabla g)\le L_U+L_f\). This provides a legitimate \(L_2\) when only the original sum and the smooth part were specified.





Write the component as the difference between the differentiable sum and the known differentiable part. Gradient linearity then gives the same difference identity for its score. Applying the triangle inequality at two points yields the sum of their score constants. This supplies a legitimate \(L_2\) from regularity of the total original potential without silently assuming the component has additional differentiability.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Cancel the common additive input before Grönwall}{Cancel the common additive input before Grönwall}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-4}


For integral solutions with drifts \(g,k\), the same input \(B\), initial states \(x,y\), and \(\sup_z\|g(z)-k(z)\|\le\varepsilon\), subtract their equations. The input cancels exactly and the drift difference is at most \(L\|Y-Z\|+\varepsilon\). Integral Grönwall gives

\[\|Y_t-Z_t\|\le e^{Lt}\|x-y\|+\varepsilon\Psi(L,t),\qquad\Psi(L,t)=\begin{cases}(e^{Lt}-1)/L,&L\ne0,\\t,&L=0.\end{cases}\]

This estimate requires no differentiation of \(B\).





Subtracting the two integral equations removes the common continuous input exactly. The drift difference is decomposed into a Lipschitz state difference and a bounded score defect. Applying integral Grönwall gives the exponential initial-error term plus the defect times \(\Psi\). The definition of \(\Psi\) includes the zero-constant case, so the formula remains valid without division by a possibly zero Lipschitz constant.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Construct both actual systems and quantify the smoothing error}{Construct both actual systems and quantify the smoothing error}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-5}


For this result, additionally assume \(g_2\) is differentiable with an \(L_2\)-Lipschitz gradient, while retaining convexity, the lower bound and the global value Lipschitz constant \(K\). The supporting subgradient at a differentiability point must equal the actual gradient. Proximal optimality therefore gives the exact identity


\[
\nabla g_2^\eta(v)=\nabla g_2(P_\eta(v)).
\]


Nonexpansiveness of \(P_\eta\) preserves the smooth-score constant \(L_2\), and \(\|v-P_\eta(v)\|\le\eta K\) gives the finite score defect


\[
\|\nabla g_2(v)-\nabla g_2^\eta(v)\|\le\eta L_2K.
\]


No asymptotic expansion or integrated remainder is used. If the original sum \(U=g_1+g_2\) is differentiable with \(L_U\)-Lipschitz gradient and \(g_1\) has constant \(L_1\), the component regularity is derived with \(L_2=L_U+L_1\).



The development constructs original and split-smoothed L\ensuremath{{}^{2}} integral solutions with the same initialization and additive input. Their input cancels. The actual drift operators differ by at most \(2\eta L_2K\), and their common gradient constant is at most \(L_1+L_2\), independently of \(\eta\). Grönwall gives


\[
\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|Y_t-Z_t\|_{L^2}
 \le 2\eta L_2K\,\Psi(2(L_1+L_2),T),
\qquad
\Psi(a,T)=\begin{cases}(e^{aT}-1)/a,&a>0,\\T,&a=0.\end{cases}
\]


Specialize the shared initialization to a constant parameter and the shared input to the latent-only Brownian construction to obtain the usual deterministic-parameter mean-field systems. The proved norm is \textbf{the supremum over time of L\ensuremath{{}^{2}} norms}. It is not the expectation of a pathwise supremum in Proposition C.3. This is a consistent split-smoothing replacement with explicit assumptions and a finite linear-in-\(\eta\) norm bound.






Both actual processes are constructed using the same initial square-integrable variable and continuous additive input. Their averaged drift maps have common Lipschitz constant at most \(2(L_1+L_2)\) and uniform difference at most \(2\eta L_2K\). The initial-error term vanishes. The resulting bound is uniform over time outside the \(L^2\) norm; moving that supremum inside expectation would assert a stronger quantity not established by this argument.
