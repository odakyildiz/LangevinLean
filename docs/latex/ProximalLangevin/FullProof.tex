\chapter{Assumptions and main results}
\section{\texorpdfstring{MYIPLA: parameter error}{MYIPLA: parameter error}}


Write \(U=g_1+g_2\) on \(\mathbb R^{d_\theta+d_x}\). Assume \(g_1\) is differentiable, \(m\)-strongly convex for \(m>0\), and has \(L_1\)-Lipschitz gradient. Assume \(g_2\) is finite-valued, convex, bounded below, and \(K\)-Lipschitz. Define its proximal map and Moreau envelope by


\[P_\eta(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p\left\{g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}\right\},\quad
g_2^\eta(x)=g_2(P_\eta(x))+\frac{\|P_\eta(x)-x\|^2}{2\eta},\]
\[V_\eta=g_1+g_2^\eta,\qquad L_\eta=L_1+\eta^{-1},\qquad\eta>0.\]


Let \(N\ge1\), \(d_\theta,d_x\ge0\), and assume the initial particle state in scaled coordinates has a finite second moment. The Gaussian vectors are independent over coordinates and time and are independent of the initial state. Denote the initial distribution in these coordinates by \(\rho_0\), and denote the Gibbs measure associated with the sum of the \(N\) copies of \(V_\eta\) by \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\). Let \(\theta_U\) and \(\theta_\eta\) maximize the parameter marginals corresponding to \(g_1+g_2\) and \(V_\eta\). The proof establishes the existence of both maximizers under these assumptions.



Precisely, the scaled state, lifted potential, and normalized target are


\[z=(\theta,x^1/\sqrt N,\ldots,x^N/\sqrt N),\qquad
W_{N,\eta}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^N V_\eta(\theta,\sqrt N z_i),\]
\[\Pi_{N,\eta}(dz)=Z_{N,\eta}^{-1}e^{-W_{N,\eta}(z)}dz.\]


A marginal maximizer of a pair potential \(V\) maximizes \(\theta\mapsto\int e^{-V(\theta,x)}dx\).



MYIPLA is the Gaussian IPLA recursion for \(V_\eta\), using the proved identity \(\nabla g_2^\eta=(I-P_\eta)/\eta\):


\[z_{n+1}=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla W_{N,\eta}(z_n)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\,\xi_n.\]


For \(0<\gamma\le2/(m+L_\eta)\) and every \(n\in\mathbb N\), the parameter iterate satisfies


\[\begin{aligned}
\bigl(\mathbb E\|\theta_n-\theta_U\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}
\le{}&\sqrt{\frac{\eta K^2}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}
+(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\eta})\\
&+\frac{33}{20}\frac{L_\eta}{m}\sqrt{d_x+\frac{d_\theta}{N}}\sqrt\gamma.
\end{aligned}\]


The first term bounds \(\|\theta_U-\theta_\eta\|\). The second bounds the root mean square distance from a parameter drawn from \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\) to \(\theta_\eta\). The remaining two terms bound the distance between the numerical distribution and \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\). The smoothing estimate is \(O(\sqrt\eta)\), and the discretization term depends on \(\eta\) through \(L_\eta\). These are the bounds proved here; the paper's linear-in-\(\eta\) smoothing expression is not a conclusion of this argument.

\section{\texorpdfstring{PIPGLA: parameter error}{PIPGLA: parameter error}}

\[P_\eta(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p\left\{g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}\right\},\quad

Let \(N\ge1\), \(d_\theta,d_x\ge0\), and assume the initial particle state in scaled coordinates has a finite second moment. The Gaussian vectors are independent over coordinates and time and are independent of the initial state. Denote the initial distribution in these coordinates by \(\rho_0\), and denote the Gibbs measure associated with the sum of the \(N\) copies of \(V_\eta\) by \(\Pi_{N,\eta}\). Let \(\theta_U\) and \(\theta_\eta\) maximize the parameter marginals corresponding to \(g_1+g_2\) and \(V_\eta\). The proof establishes the existence of both maximizers under these assumptions.



Precisely, the scaled state, lifted potential, and normalized target are


\[z=(\theta,x^1/\sqrt N,\ldots,x^N/\sqrt N),\qquad
W_{N,\eta}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^N V_\eta(\theta,\sqrt N z_i),\]
\[\Pi_{N,\eta}(dz)=Z_{N,\eta}^{-1}e^{-W_{N,\eta}(z)}dz.\]


A marginal maximizer of a pair potential \(V\) maximizes \(\theta\mapsto\int e^{-V(\theta,x)}dx\).



For PIPGLA assume also \(m\le L_1\), take any proximal parameter \(\lambda>0\), and \(0<\gamma\le2/(m+L_1)\). First apply the smooth-gradient step with its averaged parameter drift and Gaussian innovation:


\[\begin{aligned}
\widetilde\theta_n&=\theta_n-\frac\gamma N\sum_i\nabla_\theta g_1(\theta_n,x_n^i)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\,\xi_n^0,\\
\widetilde x_n^i&=x_n^i-\gamma\nabla_x g_1(\theta_n,x_n^i)+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\xi_n^i.
\end{aligned}\]


Then set \((a_i,b_i)=P_\lambda(\widetilde\theta_n,\widetilde x_n^i)\), \(\theta_{n+1}=N^{-1}\sum_i a_i\), and \(x_{n+1}^i=b_i\). Let \(\theta_\gamma\) maximize the marginal for \(V_\gamma\). The actual iterates satisfy


\[\begin{aligned}
\bigl(\mathbb E\|\theta_n-\theta_U\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}
\le{}&\frac{K|\lambda-\gamma|}{m\gamma}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma K^2}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}\\
&+\sqrt{(1-m\gamma)^n W_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\gamma})^2+\frac\gamma N C_N}.
\end{aligned}\]


All constants are explicit. Put \(D=d_\theta+Nd_x\), \(a=Nm\), \(b=NL_1\), \(c=NK\),


\[G=\sqrt{2b^2D/a+2c^2}+c,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{2D},\]
\[C_N=\frac{4b^2}{a^2}\left(\frac{G^2}{a}+\sigma^2\right)+\frac2a(Gc+c^2).\]


The first term bounds the difference caused by using \(P_\lambda\) in place of \(P_\gamma\). The next two terms bound smoothing of the parameter maximizer and the second moment of the smoothed Gibbs parameter marginal. The square-root term follows from a coupling of the splitting recursion with a stationary comparison process, followed by a recurrence for their squared distance. The constants in this estimate differ from those printed in the paper.

The Gaussian vectors are independent over coordinates and time and are independent of the initial state. Precisely, the scaled state, lifted potential, and normalized target are


\[z=(\theta,x^1/\sqrt N,\ldots,x^N/\sqrt N),\qquad
W_{N,\eta}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^N V_\eta(\theta,\sqrt N z_i),\]
\[\Pi_{N,\eta}(dz)=Z_{N,\eta}^{-1}e^{-W_{N,\eta}(z)}dz.\]


A marginal maximizer of a pair potential \(V\) maximizes \(\theta\mapsto\int e^{-V(\theta,x)}dx\).



MYPGD has the MYIPLA drift, with zero Gaussian noise in the parameter coordinates and unchanged Gaussian noise in the latent coordinates:


\[z_{n+1}=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla W_{N,\eta}(z_n)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\,M\xi_n,\]


where \(M\) is the orthogonal projection onto latent coordinates. Assume \(m\le L_\eta\) explicitly and \(0<\gamma\le2/(m+L_\eta)\). For every \(n\in\mathbb N\), the actual finite-particle recursion satisfies


\[\begin{aligned}
\bigl(\mathbb E\|\theta_n^{\rm PGD}-\theta_U\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}
\le{}&\sqrt{\frac{2d_\theta}{Nm}}+\sqrt{\frac{\eta K^2}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}\\
&+(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\eta})\\
&+\frac{33}{20}\frac{L_\eta}{m}\sqrt{d_x+\frac{d_\theta}{N}}\sqrt\gamma.
\end{aligned}\]


The additional \(\sqrt{2d_\theta/(Nm)}\) bounds the distance caused by setting the parameter noise to zero. It is obtained by comparing the two recursions with the same latent Gaussian variables. The displayed estimate concerns the finite-particle algorithm; it does not establish the paper's stronger estimate for the mean-field distance or its constants.

\chapter{Mathematical argument}
\section{\texorpdfstring{Proximal optimality and differentiability of the envelope}{Proximal optimality and differentiability of the envelope}}



For \(\eta>0\), add quadratic penalty \(\|p-x\|^2/(2\eta)\) to the finite convex function \(g_2(p)\). Under the stated lower bound the objective is coercive and strictly convex, so it has unique minimizer \(P_\eta(x)\). Its residual support at two inputs and subgradient monotonicity give

\[\|P_\eta(x)-P_\eta(y)\|^2\le
\langle P_\eta(x)-P_\eta(y),x-y\rangle.\]

Expansion of residual differences also makes \(I-P_\eta\) nonexpansive. Compare the envelope objective at \(x+u\) using its minimizer at \(x\), and conversely. The resulting two-sided quadratic remainder proves

\[\nabla g_2^\eta(x)=\frac{x-P_\eta(x)}\eta.\]

Residual nonexpansiveness gives gradient constant \(\eta^{-1}\). Convexity of the envelope means \(V_\eta=g_1+g_2^\eta\) retains curvature \(m\) and has gradient constant \(L_\eta=L_1+\eta^{-1}\). These established properties meet the Gaussian Euler transport theorem \cite[Section~3.45.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.


\section{\texorpdfstring{The finite Moreau potential-gap estimate}{The finite Moreau potential-gap estimate}}



Competitor \(p=x\) in the minimizing objective gives \(g_2^\eta(x)\le g_2(x)\). Conversely the value Lipschitz bound gives, for all \(p\),

\[g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}
\ge g_2(x)-K\|p-x\|+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\eta}
\ge g_2(x)-\frac{\eta K^2}{2}.\]

The last step completes a scalar square in \(\|p-x\|\). Evaluation at the proximal minimizer therefore gives

\[V_\eta\le U\le V_\eta+\delta_\eta,\qquad\delta_\eta=\eta K^2/2.\]

Exponentiation reverses potential order and latent integration preserves the resulting nonnegative-function order. Taking negative logarithms of the positive finite marginal integrals gives, for \(F_U,F_\eta\), the relation \(F_\eta\le F_U\le F_\eta+\delta_\eta\). The function \(U=g_1+g_2\) is continuous because \(g_1\) is differentiable and \(g_2\) is Lipschitz. The comparison gives Gaussian domination locally uniform in the parameter; dominated convergence therefore proves continuity of the original marginal. Its decay as the parameter norm tends to infinity gives existence of a marginal maximizer.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Marginal-potential comparison and center displacement}{Marginal-potential comparison and center displacement}}



Let \(\theta_U\) and \(\theta_\eta\) minimize \(F_U\) and \(F_\eta\). Marginal strong convexity \cite[Section~3.12.3]{LangevinLean2026IPLA} makes \(F_\eta\) \(m\)-strongly convex. Quadratic growth, the uniform gap, and minimality of \(\theta_U\) imply

\[\begin{aligned}
\frac m2\|\theta_U-\theta_\eta\|^2
&\le F_\eta(\theta_U)-F_\eta(\theta_\eta)\\
&\le F_U(\theta_U)-F_\eta(\theta_\eta)\\
&\le F_U(\theta_\eta)-F_\eta(\theta_\eta)
\le\delta_\eta.
\end{aligned}\]

Taking square roots yields \(\|\theta_U-\theta_\eta\|\le K\sqrt{\eta/m}\) for every positive \(\eta\). The argument does not differentiate the original nonsmooth potential. Its order is \(\sqrt\eta\); the proved result therefore does not support replacing this term by a linear smoothing rate.


\section{\texorpdfstring{The MYIPLA bound about the original marginal maximizer}{The MYIPLA bound about the original marginal maximizer}}



MYIPLA equals IPLA for \(V_\eta\). The summed-potential statements \cite[Section~3.1.3]{LangevinLean2026IPLA} and \cite[Section~3.1.4]{LangevinLean2026IPLA} give curvature \(Nm\), gradient constant \(NL_\eta\), and dimension \(D=d_\theta+Nd_x\). Step \(\gamma/N\) gives contraction \(1-m\gamma\). Apply Gaussian Euler transport \cite[Section~3.45.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC} and parameter projection \cite[Section~3.48.9]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.



The sharp marginal estimate Section~\ref{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpIPLA-lean-1} gives radius \(\sqrt{d_\theta/(Nm)}\). Minkowski adds the center displacement. The four contributions are

\[K\sqrt{\eta/m},\quad\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}},\quad
(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\eta}),\quad
\frac{33}{20}\frac{L_\eta}{m}\sqrt{\gamma\left(d_x+\frac{d_\theta}{N}\right)}.\]

A smaller \(\eta\) reduces the first contribution while increasing \(L_\eta\) and restricting the admissible step. Thus smoothing and discretization choices must satisfy the displayed joint conditions. The estimate is for the stated Gaussian particle law.


\section{\texorpdfstring{The equal-scale PIPGLA transport estimate}{The equal-scale PIPGLA transport estimate}}



Take \(\lambda=\gamma\). Apply the smooth noisy forward update and then the pairwise proximal maps, averaging their parameter outputs. Couple its noise with the stationary Langevin reference for \(V_\gamma\) supplied by \cite[Section~3.42.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}. Keep the proximal support inner products in the squared-error expansion until their energy terms are combined.



Put \(s=\gamma/N\), \(a=Nm\), \(b=NL_1\), and \(c=NK\). Drift and noise moment bounds are \(G=\sqrt{2b^2D/a+2c^2}+c\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{2D}\). The local increment is bounded by \(Gs+\sigma\sqrt s\). Squaring with \((u+v)^2\le2u^2+2v^2\) and using \(s\le1/a\) bounds the higher powers by the coefficient

\[C_N=\frac{4b^2}{a^2}\left(\frac{G^2}{a}+\sigma^2\right)+\frac2a(Gc+c^2).\]

For gap \(\delta=N\gamma K^2/2=sc^2/2\), the one-step forcing is

\[\mathcal F=(1+(as)^{-1})[bs(Gs+\sigma\sqrt s)]^2+2s\delta+2s^2Gc+s^2c^2.\]

Weighted Young controls the smooth defect. Convex support, stationarity, and zero expected pairings with future Brownian increments give \(2s\delta+2s^2Gc\) for the energy cross term. The remaining term is the proximal quadratic remainder. Under \(s\le1/a\) these estimates imply \(\mathcal F\le as^2C_N\). The recurrence coefficient is \(1-a s=1-m\gamma\), so the initial coupling infimum and geometric sum give

\[W_2(\rho_n,\Pi_{N,\gamma})^2
\le(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_{N,\gamma})^2+\frac\gamma N C_N.\]

Projection adds the sharp equilibrium radius to the square root of this transport bound, and the center comparison adds \(K\sqrt{\gamma/m}\). Since the recurrence controls squared transport, its transient factor occurs inside the final square root.


\section{\texorpdfstring{PIPGLA comparison at two positive proximal scales}{PIPGLA comparison at two positive proximal scales}}



For a convex \(K\)-Lipschitz penalty, proximal optimality and nonexpansiveness at a shifted input give

\[\|P_\lambda(x)-P_\eta(x)\|\le K|\lambda-\eta|.\]

Apply this estimate to every pair. Jensen for the parameter average and the exact latent scaling give the same full-state bound \(K|\lambda-\eta|\) per step. Gradient-step contraction \cite[Section~3.10.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC} has factor \(q=1-m\gamma\), and proximal nonexpansiveness preserves it when both chains use the same innovation.



From common initialization, geometric summation bounds their difference by \(K|\lambda-\eta|/(1-q)=K|\lambda-\eta|/(m\gamma)\) uniformly over finite iteration counts. Set \(\eta=\gamma\) and combine this comparison with the equal-scale bound. Minkowski adds the same constant to the parameter root mean square error.


\section{\texorpdfstring{The finite-particle MYPGD–MYIPLA comparison}{The finite-particle MYPGD–MYIPLA comparison}}



Extend any finite-cost coupling of the current MYPGD and MYIPLA laws by an independent standard Gaussian vector. Both updates use its latent component; only MYIPLA uses its parameter component. That omitted component is centered and has squared \(L^2\) norm \(2\gamma d_\theta/N\). Independence makes its mixed expectation with the deterministic discrepancy zero.



The smooth contraction theorem \cite[Section~3.10.7]{LangevinLean2026ULASC} gives factor \(1-m\gamma\). The constructed output coupling thus has squared cost at most \((1-m\gamma)^2\) times the input squared cost plus the omitted variance. Taking the input coupling infimum, for \(E_n=W_2(\rho_n^{\mathrm{PGD}},\rho_n^{\mathrm{MYIPLA}})^2\), gives

\[E_{n+1}\le(1-m\gamma)^2E_n+\frac{2\gamma d_\theta}{N},\qquad E_0=0.\]

The initial law is common, and finite moments justify all transport squares. Step admissibility gives \(0\le m\gamma\le1\) and therefore \(1-(1-m\gamma)^2=m\gamma(2-m\gamma)\ge m\gamma\). The geometric sum gives \(E_n\le2d_\theta/(Nm)\). Parameter contraction \cite[Section~3.1.6]{LangevinLean2026IPLA} and projected root mean square inequality \cite[Section~3.48.9]{LangevinLean2026ULASC} consequently add \(\sqrt{2d_\theta/(Nm)}\) to the MYIPLA parameter bound.



The comparison radius is a finite-particle upper bound and remains for finite \(N\). This result does not assert the stronger mean-field metric estimate; mean-field integral constructions and their particle comparisons are established separately.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Exact maps for MYIPLA, PIPULA and PIPGLA}{Exact maps for MYIPLA, PIPULA and PIPGLA}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean}


MYIPLA, PIPULA, and PIPGLA use the same scaled parameter–particle space but apply drift, proximal maps, and Gaussian perturbations in different orders. Define these update maps first for arbitrary deterministic inputs. The residual identity identifies MYIPLA and its PIPULA specialization with Euler updates for suitable envelopes. PIPGLA applies proximity after the noisy drift and therefore requires its own splitting estimate.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.4]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The averaged pair map in coordinates}{The averaged pair map in coordinates}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-1}


Define the parameter and latent coordinates of the pair map by \(\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)_\theta=N^{-1}\sum_i(P(J_i z))_\theta\) and \(\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)_{x_i}=N^{-1/2}(P(J_i z))_x\). Every pair is evaluated at the same preceding parameter. The proposed parameters are averaged, and each proposed latent coordinate returns to its own block.



The coefficient \(1/N\) averages the common parameter, while \(1/\sqrt N\) converts each physical latent output to a scaled coordinate. These definitions specify one shared parameter rather than separate evolving parameter copies. They do not assert minimization of a full-state proximal objective.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The order of drift, proximity, and noise}{The order of drift, proximity, and noise}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-2}


PIPGLA first takes the noisy gradient step for \(g_1\) and then applies the pairwise proximal map:

\[
y_n=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla\!\left(\sum_i g_1(J_i z_n)\right)
 +\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{N}}\xi_n,
\qquad z_{n+1}=\operatorname{Pair}_{P_\lambda}(y_n).
\]

PIPULA is the MYIPLA specialization with \(g_1=0\) and \(\lambda=\gamma\), in which proximity precedes the added Gaussian perturbation. The expanded MYIPLA update is

\[T_{\mathrm{MY}}(z,\xi)=(1-\gamma/\eta)z-\frac\gamma N\nabla F_{g_1}(z)+\frac\gamma\eta\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)+\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\xi.\]

In MYIPLA the pairwise proximal value is evaluated at the preceding state and contributes through an explicit residual drift. In PIPGLA it is evaluated at the already perturbed smooth step. These compositions give different outputs for the same innovation. Their update identities must therefore be proved separately. In the source convergence analysis PIPGLA is compared with the original nonsmooth Gibbs model; the MYIPLA theorem cannot be applied merely by changing the update’s name.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.1.7]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Recursion with a supplied innovation sequence}{Recursion with a supplied innovation sequence}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-3}


For an update function \(T\), define \(Z_0=z_0\) and \(Z_{n+1}=T(Z_n,\xi_n)\). Induction determines a unique finite-time sequence from the initial state and innovation sequence. Substitution of the three update maps defines the three particle algorithms.



This deterministic definition requires no Gaussian law or measurability assumption. When those properties and input independence are imposed, the same finite recursion becomes a measurable function of the inputs and defines its probability law. This permits the update equalities to be used on any probability space chosen for a coupling argument.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Equality of PIPULA and the specified MYIPLA specialization}{Equality of PIPULA and the specified MYIPLA specialization}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-4}


The PIPULA parameter update is \(N^{-1}\sum_i(P(J_i z))_\theta+a\xi_\theta\). Specialize MYIPLA to \(g_1=0\) and \(\eta=\gamma\ne0\). The smooth gradient vanishes, \(1-\gamma/\gamma=0\) removes the old-state term, and \(\gamma/\gamma=1\) gives the pairwise proximal term with coefficient one. Thus the two updates agree for every state and innovation.



Induction in the common recursion gives equality of every finite iterate. Any probability law on the inputs consequently gives equal output laws. The identity uses equality and positivity of the relevant scales, rather than a limiting approximation.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The physical parameter and latent-coordinate equations}{The physical parameter and latent-coordinate equations}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-5}


The lifted smooth gradient has parameter block \(\sum_i\nabla_\theta g_1(J_i z)\) and latent block \(\sqrt N\nabla_x g_1(J_i z)\). Substitute these formulas into the state update. Multiplication of a latent coordinate by \(\sqrt N\) changes the Gaussian coefficient to \(\sqrt{2\gamma}\) and the smooth drift coefficient to \(\gamma\), using \(\sqrt N\sqrt{2\gamma/N}=\sqrt{2\gamma}\).



The parameter equation contains the average smooth force and average proximal parameter output. The physical latent equation contains the force and proximal output of its own pair. Multiplication by \(\sqrt N\) affects only the latent block. The resulting equations have parameter variance divided by particle count and the usual physical latent variance.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.1.5]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The residual identity and the smoothed Euler update}{The residual identity and the smoothed Euler update}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Algorithms-lean-6}


For a joint-pair map \(P\), apply \(P\) to each \(J_i z\), average the parameter outputs, and divide the physical latent outputs by \(\sqrt N\). This retains one common parameter rather than \(N\) parameter variables. For \(P=P_\lambda\), MYIPLA is the Euler update for \(V_\lambda=g_1+g_2^\lambda\) because

\[
\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)=z-\frac\lambda N\nabla\!\left(\sum_i g_2^\lambda(J_i z)\right).
\]

Substitution into the expanded update cancels the extra multiples of \(z\) and gives

\[
z_{n+1}=z_n-\frac\gamma N\nabla\mathcal V_\lambda(z_n)
 +\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{N}}\,\xi_n.
\]

The parameter and physical latent Gaussian coefficients are \(\sqrt{2\gamma/N}\) and \(\sqrt{2\gamma}\). More explicitly, substituting \(P_\eta(v)=v-\eta\nabla g_2^\eta(v)\) in each pair makes the averaged and rescaled residual exactly \((\eta/N)\nabla F_{g_2^\eta}\). Addition of the smooth gradient then gives the force of \(g_1+g_2^\eta\). The Gaussian perturbation is unchanged. Equality of the complete chains follows by induction from equality of the update maps.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Proximal minimizers and subgradient optimality}{Proximal minimizers and subgradient optimality}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean}


On a real inner-product space, define \(Q_{\lambda,x}(p)=f(p)+\|p-x\|^2/(2\lambda)\). A proximal point minimizes this objective globally. In finite dimension, convexity, a lower bound for the function, and \(\lambda>0\) give existence by compact attainment. The scaled residual is a subgradient at the minimizer; subgradient monotonicity then proves uniqueness and firm nonexpansiveness. The variational statements also apply to any point already known to minimize the objective.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The objective and global subgradient supports}{The objective and global subgradient supports}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-1}


The condition \(p\in\arg\min Q_{\lambda,x}\) means global minimization of the quadratic-penalized objective. The support condition \(s\in\partial f(p)\) means that \(f(p)+\langle s,y-p\rangle\le f(y)\) for every competitor \(y\). Apply these global support inequalities at \((p,s)\) and \((q,t)\), taking each support point as the competitor for the other. Addition cancels the function values and gives \(\langle s-t,p-q\rangle\ge0\).



The resulting inequality \(0\le\langle s-t,p-q\rangle\) is monotonicity of the subdifferential. Its proof uses only the support inequalities and inner-product algebra, and remains valid at nondifferentiable points of a convex function.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The residual characterizes the minimum}{The residual characterizes the minimum}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-2}


A vector \(s\) supports \(g_2\) at \(p\) when \(g_2(p)+\langle s,y-p\rangle\le g_2(y)\) for every \(y\). For positive proximal parameter, minimization is equivalent to the residual support condition

\[
p=P_\lambda(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{x-p}{\lambda}\in\partial g_2(p).
\]

For necessity, compare the objective at a minimizer \(p\) with its value at \(p+t(y-p)\). Convexity bounds the function increment, and division by positive \(t\) followed by a limit gives the support inequality. For sufficiency, substitute that support in the difference of the quadratic objectives. The remaining squared norm is nonnegative, proving global minimality without differentiability of \(g_2\).



Monotonicity applied to the two residual supports gives

\[
\|P_\lambda(x)-P_\lambda(y)\|^2
\le\langle x-y,P_\lambda(x)-P_\lambda(y)\rangle.
\]

Cauchy–Schwarz proves nonexpansiveness of \(P_\lambda\). Expansion of the residual squared difference proves nonexpansiveness of \(I-P_\lambda\). To state the necessity calculation explicitly, fix competitor \(y\) and put \(d=y-p\). Compare the objective at \(p\) and \(p+td\) for \(0<t\le1\). Convexity bounds \(f(p+td)\) by \((1-t)f(p)+tf(y)\). Expansion of the quadratic and division by \(t\) gives

\[f(y)-f(p)\ge\left\langle\frac{x-p}{\lambda},d\right\rangle-\frac{t}{2\lambda}\|d\|^2.\]

Letting \(t\downarrow0\) proves the support. Conversely the same support gives \(Q_{\lambda,x}(y)-Q_{\lambda,x}(p)\ge\|y-p\|^2/(2\lambda)\) at \(p\), quantitatively bounding the objective increase away from \(p\). Thus the support condition proves both optimality and quadratic growth of the penalized objective.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Uniqueness and nonexpansiveness of the proximal and residual maps}{Uniqueness and nonexpansiveness of the proximal and residual maps}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-3}


Apply subgradient monotonicity to \((x-p)/\lambda\) and \((y-q)/\lambda\). Multiplication by positive \(\lambda\) and expansion give \(\|p-q\|^2\le\langle x-y,p-q\rangle\). For equal inputs this forces \(p=q\), proving uniqueness. Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\|p-q\|\le\|x-y\|\). Expanding \(\|(x-y)-(p-q)\|^2\) and using the same firm inequality gives \(\|(x-p)-(y-q)\|\le\|x-y\|\).



In the notation \(u=x-y\) and \(v=p-q\), firmness states \(\langle u,v\rangle\ge\|v\|^2\). Therefore \(\|u-v\|^2\le\|u\|^2-\|v\|^2\le\|u\|^2\). This proves the unit Lipschitz bound for the residual map \(I-P_\lambda\). For equal inputs, \(v=0\) gives uniqueness separately from the existence argument.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Existence by compact minimization}{Existence by compact minimization}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Proximal-lean-4}


Assume \(\lambda>0\) and define

\[
Q_{\lambda,x}(p)=g_2(p)+\frac{\|p-x\|^2}{2\lambda},\qquad
P_\lambda(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_p Q_{\lambda,x}(p).
\]

A finite convex function on a finite-dimensional real space is continuous. If \(g_2\ge b\), the positive quadratic term forces \(Q_{\lambda,x}(p)\) above the competitor value \(Q_{\lambda,x}(x)=g_2(x)\) outside a large ball. A suitable radius is \(\sqrt{2\lambda(g_2(x)-b)}+1\). Compactness and continuity give a minimizer inside that ball, and the outside bound makes it global.



More precisely, center the ball at \(x\) and choose it to contain every point whose objective can be smaller than the value at \(x\). The lower bound on the function and positivity of the quadratic coefficient give this containment. The compact minimizer is therefore also a global minimizer, and the residual support condition proves that it is unique.




\section{\texorpdfstring{Moreau envelope: gradient, smoothness and finite bias}{Moreau envelope: gradient, smoothness and finite bias}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean}


Let \(f\) be finite, convex, and bounded below on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, and let \(\lambda>0\). Set \(P=P_\lambda\), \(f^\lambda(x)=Q_{\lambda,x}(P x)\), and \(r(x)=(x-Px)/\lambda\). Comparison of minimizing objectives at neighboring inputs gives a two-sided quadratic remainder, proving that the envelope gradient is the scaled proximal residual. A global Lipschitz bound on the original function also bounds the residual and the uniform difference between the original function and its envelope.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Value bounds and the exact residual}{Value bounds and the exact residual}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-1}


The envelope is the minimum objective value. Using competitor \(p=x\) gives \(f^\lambda(x)\le f(x)\). If \(f\ge b\), nonnegativity of the quadratic term gives \(f^\lambda\ge b\). These comparisons prove that the envelope is finite and satisfies the same lower bound at every input.



The residual at a minimizing point is a subgradient of the original function. Identification with the derivative of the envelope requires the two neighboring-objective comparisons below; it does not follow from the value bounds alone.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Global supports and quadratic upper remainder}{Global supports and quadratic upper remainder}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-2}


Use the residual support at \(P x\) with competitor \(P y\). Addition of the two quadratic penalties leaves a nonnegative squared norm, giving \(f^\lambda(x)+\langle r(x),y-x\rangle\le f^\lambda(y)\). In the other direction, use \(P x\) as competitor for the objective at \(y\). Expanding its penalty gives upper remainder \(\|y-x\|^2/(2\lambda)\).



For \(z=ax+by\), apply the global envelope supports based at \(z\) to the two endpoints. Multiply by weights \(a,b\ge0\) satisfying \(a+b=1\). Their linear terms cancel, proving convexity. The lower comparison uses the support of the original function at \(P_\lambda x\), whereas the upper comparison uses only the old minimizer as an admissible competitor. Its quadratic remainder is \(\|y-x\|^2/(2\lambda)\).


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Nonexpansiveness and the derivative}{Nonexpansiveness and the derivative}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-3}


Define \(g_2^\lambda(x)=Q_{\lambda,x}(P_\lambda(x))\) and \(r_\lambda(x)=\lambda^{-1}(x-P_\lambda(x))\). The residual support gives the lower comparison and the old minimizer as competitor gives the upper comparison:

\[
0\le g_2^\lambda(y)-g_2^\lambda(x)
 -\langle r_\lambda(x),y-x\rangle
\le\frac{\|y-x\|^2}{2\lambda}.
\]

The upper remainder is \(o(\|y-x\|)\), so \(\nabla g_2^\lambda=r_\lambda\). The global supports also give convexity. Nonexpansiveness of \(I-P_\lambda\) yields

\[
\|\nabla g_2^\lambda(x)-\nabla g_2^\lambda(y)\|
\le\lambda^{-1}\|x-y\|.
\]

For \(y=x+h\), the remainder divided by \(\|h\|\) is bounded by \(\|h\|/(2\lambda)\), which tends to zero. Thus the Fréchet derivative is \(h\mapsto\langle r_\lambda(x),h\rangle\). The residual-map inequality gives gradient Lipschitz constant \(1/\lambda\), hence continuity of the gradient. These facts justify smooth Langevin analysis for the envelope even when the original function is nonsmooth.



\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Bounded residual and a sharp uniform gap}{Bounded residual and a sharp uniform gap}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Moreau-lean-4}


Assume \(f\) is convex, bounded below, and globally \(K\)-Lipschitz, with \(K\ge0\) and \(\lambda>0\). At input \(x\), write \(p=P_\lambda(x)\) and \(r=(x-p)/\lambda\). The optimality condition gives \(r\in\partial f(p)\). Test this support at \(p+r\) and apply the Lipschitz bound:

\[
\|r\|^2\le f(p+r)-f(p)\le K\|r\|.
\]

For \(r=0\) the residual bound is immediate; otherwise cancellation of its positive norm gives \(\|r\|\le K\). From \(x-p=\lambda r\) one obtains \(\|x-p\|\le\lambda K\).



To bound the value gap, competitor \(p=x\) gives \(f^\lambda(x)\le f(x)\). Set \(d=\|p-x\|\) and subtract the minimizing objective value:

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x)-f^\lambda(x)
&=f(x)-f(p)-\frac{d^2}{2\lambda}\\
&\le Kd-\frac{d^2}{2\lambda}\\
&=\frac{\lambda K^2}{2}-\frac{(d-\lambda K)^2}{2\lambda}
\le\frac{\lambda K^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The squared remainder has a positive denominator because \(\lambda>0\). Together with the upper comparison this proves \(0\le f(x)-f^\lambda(x)\le\lambda K^2/2\) at every input. For \(U=g_1+f\) and \(V_\lambda=g_1+f^\lambda\) the same estimate becomes \(V_\lambda\le U\le V_\lambda+\lambda K^2/2\). Exponentiation and latent integration then compare marginal potentials by the same finite gap. No asymptotic expansion or interchange of an unbounded remainder with integration is required.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Uniform potential gaps and marginal-center displacement}{Uniform potential gaps and marginal-center displacement}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Perturbation-lean}


Assume one potential lies between a smooth strongly convex potential and that potential plus a finite constant. Exponentiation and integration preserve a multiplicative comparison of the latent marginals; negative logarithms then give the same additive constant between marginal potentials. Strong-convexity growth at the comparison minimizer converts this value difference into a quantitative bound on the distance between marginal maximizers. Gaussian domination also proves existence of the original marginal maximizer.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Quadratic growth from strong convexity and minimality}{Quadratic growth from strong convexity and minimality}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Perturbation-lean-1}


Let \(F\) be \(m\)-strongly convex with \(m\ge0\) and global minimizer \(z\). For any \(x\) set

\[
A=\frac m2\|x-z\|^2,\qquad G=F(x)-F(z).
\]

Both quantities are nonnegative. For \(0<t<1\), the convex-combination inequality between \(z\) and \(x\) and minimality give

\[
F(z)\le F((1-t)z+tx)
\le (1-t)F(z)+tF(x)-t(1-t)A.
\]

Subtract \(F(z)\) and divide by \(t\) to obtain \((1-t)A\le G\) for every admissible \(t\). If \(G<A\), then \(A>0\) and the finite choice

\[
t=\frac{A-G}{2A}\in(0,1/2]
\]

gives \((1-t)A=(A+G)/2>G\), a contradiction. Therefore \(A\le G\), or

\[
F(x)\ge F(z)+\frac m2\|x-z\|^2.
\]

The proof uses only strong convexity and global minimality, without differentiating \(F\). Applied to the comparison marginal potential, this inequality bounds squared center distance by its increase above the minimum.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Marginal-potential comparison from a uniform gap}{Marginal-potential comparison from a uniform gap}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Perturbation-lean-2}


Suppose \(U\) is continuous and \(V\) is differentiable and \(m\)-strongly convex, with \(m>0\), and assume \(V\le U\le V+\delta\). Exponentiation reverses order, and integration over each latent fiber gives

\[
e^{-\delta}k_V(\theta)\le k_U(\theta)\le k_V(\theta),
\qquad k_W(\theta)=\int e^{-W(\theta,x)}dx.
\]

The strongly convex comparison \(V\) gives positive finite slice integrals and a Gaussian bound that is integrable in the latent variable, locally uniformly in the parameter. The displayed inequalities give positivity, finiteness, and decay of the original marginal as the parameter norm tends to infinity. Continuity of \(U\) and dominated convergence give continuity of that marginal. Its positivity, continuity, and decay then imply that it attains a maximum. Taking negative logarithms gives

\[
F_V(\theta)\le F_U(\theta)\le F_V(\theta)+\delta,
\qquad F_W=-\log k_W.
\]

Let \(\theta_U\) minimize \(F_U\) and let \(\theta_V\) minimize the \(m\)-strongly convex potential \(F_V\). The potential bounds and minimality give \(F_V(\theta_U)-F_V(\theta_V)\le\delta\). The quadratic-growth inequality therefore yields

\[
\frac m2\|\theta_U-\theta_V\|^2\le\delta.
\]

In the equivalent notation \(V\le U\le V+\delta\), the pointwise weights satisfy \(e^{-\delta}e^{-V}\le e^{-U}\le e^{-V}\), and integration gives \(e^{-\delta}M_V(\theta)\le M_U(\theta)\le M_V(\theta)\). The positive finite comparison marginal makes \(M_U\) positive and finite. Thus logarithmic monotonicity legitimately yields \(F_V\le F_U\le F_V+\delta\) with \(F=-\log M\). The additive gap is still \(\delta\) because the logarithm is taken after integration. No differentiation of the original marginal or integration of an asymptotic remainder is used.

On positive finite marginal densities, \(-\log\) reverses order. Thus an original likelihood maximizer minimizes \(F_U\). At the two minimizers \(\theta_U,\theta_V\), the sandwich gives \(F_V(\theta_U)\le F_U(\theta_U)\le F_U(\theta_V)\le F_V(\theta_V)+\delta\). \section{\texorpdfstring{Sharp Gibbs concentration by dilation}{Sharp Gibbs concentration by dilation}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean}


Let \(F\) be continuous and \(q\)-strongly convex in dimension \(d\), with \(q>0\) and global minimizer \(z\). A contraction toward the minimizer compares the Gibbs partition with its unnormalized second moment. The left derivative at unit dilation gives the dimension-over-curvature bound. Gaussian domination ensures all integrals are finite, and only an explicit scalar expression is differentiated; no derivative of the potential is required.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The partition comparison under radial contraction}{The partition comparison under radial contraction}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean-1}


Strong convexity and minimality give \(F(z+r(x-z))\le F(x)-\frac q2(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2\) for \(0<r<1\). Exponentiate and use \(e^u\ge1+u\). The dilation \(x\mapsto z+r(x-z)\) has determinant \(r^d\), so integration gives

\[Z+\frac q2(1-r^2)M\le r^{-d}Z,\qquad Z=\int e^{-F},\quad M=\int\|x-z\|^2e^{-F}.\]

In the notation \(w(x)=e^{-F(x)}\), \(Z=\int w\), \(M=\int\|x-z\|^2w(x)\,dx\), and \(d=\dim H\), Gaussian domination first proves integrability of \(w\) and \(\|x-z\|^2w\). Strong convexity combined with quadratic growth at the mode gives

\[F(z+r(x-z))\le F(x)-\tfrac q2(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2.\]

Using \(e^u\ge1+u\) after exponentiation gives

\[w(z+r(x-z))\ge w(x)+\tfrac q2(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2w(x).\]

Indeed the strong-convexity segment from the minimizer to \(x\) contributes radial deficit \(q(1-r^2)\|x-z\|^2/2\). Inequality \(e^u\ge1+u\) turns it into a density-plus-moment bound. The determinant is \(r^d\), so the integral of the dilated density is \(r^{-d}Z\). This proves the partition comparison with all terms finite.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The limiting derivative and the normalized moment}{The limiting derivative and the normalized moment}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean-2}

The left difference quotients at one are nonnegative; differentiating the explicit scalar expression gives \(\Phi'(1)=dZ-qM\ge0\) and hence \(M\le dZ/q\). The partition \(Z\) is positive and finite by Gaussian domination. Normalization therefore gives

\[\int\|x-z\|^2\pi(dx)=M/Z\le d/q,\qquad\pi(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{-F(x)}dx.\]

The argument does not differentiate \(F\). Equivalently, \(qM\le dZ\) may be divided by positive \(Z\). The normalized second moment is \(M/Z\) and is bounded by \(d/q\). This permits the estimate for continuous nonsmooth strongly convex potentials without an integration-by-parts assumption.

\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpConcentration-lean-3}


With \(Z,M,d\) from the partition comparison define

\[\Phi(r)=r^d\bigl[Z+\tfrac q2(1-r^2)M\bigr].\]

For \(0<r<1\) one has \(\Phi(r)\le Z=\Phi(1)\). Thus when \(-1<u<0\), the difference quotient \((\Phi(1+u)-\Phi(1))/u\) is nonnegative: its numerator and denominator are both nonpositive.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Sharp concentration of the parameter marginal}{Sharp concentration of the parameter marginal}}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The Gaussian particle estimate with sharp equilibrium radius}{The Gaussian particle estimate with sharp equilibrium radius}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SharpIPLA-lean-2}


Assume \(N>0\), \(\gamma>0\), \(\gamma\le2/(m+L)\), independent Gaussian innovations, and \(L^2\) initialization. The scaled Gaussian Euler estimate, parameter projection, and the sharp equilibrium moment give

\[\operatorname{RMS}_n\le\sqrt{\frac{d_\theta}{Nm}}+(1-m\gamma)^nW_2(\rho_0,\Pi_N)+\frac{33}{20}\frac Lm\sqrt{d_x+d_\theta/N}\sqrt\gamma.\]

\section{\texorpdfstring{A proximal distance–energy inequality}{A proximal distance–energy inequality}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-ProximalEnergy-lean}


Let \(f\) be convex, \(p=P_\eta(x)\) its proximal point at positive scale \(\eta\), and \(s\in\partial f(x)\) a subgradient at the input. The support at the proximal point bounds its squared distance to an arbitrary comparator. The input support then replaces the proximal function value by the input value. Completion of a square bounds the remaining term by the squared scale times the squared subgradient norm.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The support and completed-square estimates}{The support and completed-square estimates}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ProximalEnergy-lean-1}


Let \(p=P_\lambda(y)\), \(s\in\partial g_2(y)\), and comparator \(a\) be given. The residual subgradient at \(p\) supplies a support evaluated at \(a\). Evaluate the support of \(s\) at \(p\) and combine the inequalities with the squared-distance expansion. Nonnegativity of \(\|(y-p)-\lambda s\|^2\) gives the inequality corresponding to Lemma A.8 of the source paper:

\[
\|p-a\|^2\le\|y-a\|^2
 -2\lambda\bigl(g_2(y)-g_2(a)\bigr)+\lambda^2\|s\|^2.
\]

Under \(\|s\|^2\le C\), the last term is at most \(\lambda^2C\). The selected minimal subgradient in the source assumption is one possible choice; any supporting subgradient with the same norm bound suffices.



In equivalent notation, put \(d=x-p\). The support at \(p\) gives \(2\eta[f(p)-f(a)]\le2\langle d,p-a\rangle\), and the input support gives \(f(x)-f(p)\le\langle s,d\rangle\). Their addition to the distance expansion leaves \(2\eta\langle s,d\rangle-\|d\|^2\le\eta^2\|s\|^2\), which follows from \(\|d-\eta s\|^2\ge0\). This proves the energy estimate directly from proximal optimality and the input subgradient bound.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The energy inequality for the proximal map}{The energy inequality for the proximal map}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ProximalEnergy-lean-2}


The existence theorem supplies a minimizer \(P_\eta(x)\), so the variational estimate gives \(\|P_\eta(x)-a\|^2\le\|x-a\|^2-2\eta(f(x)-f(a))+\eta^2\|s\|^2\). If \(\|s\|^2\le C\), multiplication by nonnegative \(\eta^2\) bounds its remainder by \(\eta^2 C\).



The underlying expansion is \(\|p-y\|^2=\|x-y\|^2-2\langle x-p,x-y\rangle+\|x-p\|^2\). The residual support at \(p\) bounds the associated inner product by the function difference. A subgradient \(s_x\in\partial f(x)\) bounds \(f(x)-f(p)\) by \(\langle s_x,x-p\rangle\). Completing the square then gives \(2\lambda\langle s_x,x-p\rangle-\|x-p\|^2\le\lambda^2\|s_x\|^2\). Thus \(\|s_x\|^2\le C\) yields remainder \(\lambda^2C\).



The comparator is arbitrary, so a random reference point may later be substituted pointwise. A numerical bound on \(\|s\|^2\) controls only the remainder; \(s\) must still satisfy the global support condition. Positivity of the scale ensures both minimizer existence and the residual optimality characterization.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Energy estimates for averaged pairwise proximal outputs}{Energy estimates for averaged pairwise proximal outputs}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-ParticleEnergy-lean}


The pairwise algorithm averages the proposed parameter outputs and rescales the proposed latent outputs. It generally differs from a proximal minimizer of one objective on the shared state. Nevertheless Jensen’s inequality bounds its squared parameter error, and the latent squared errors satisfy exact scaling identities. Summing the single-pair proximal inequalities therefore gives the required full-state energy estimate for the stated algorithm.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Averaging squares in the shared parameter}{Averaging squares in the shared parameter}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ParticleEnergy-lean-1}


For each parameter coordinate, Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(N(N^{-1}\sum_i b_i)^2\le\sum_i b_i^2\). Each latent coordinate instead has an exact squared scaling identity after multiplication by \(N\), using \((\sqrt N)^2=N\). Adding all coordinate inequalities gives

\[\|\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)-a\|^2\le\frac1N\sum_i\|P(J_i z)-J_i a\|^2.\]\[N\left|\frac1N\sum_i p_{i,\theta,j}-a_{\theta,j}\right|^2\le\sum_i|p_{i,\theta,j}-a_{\theta,j}|^2.\]

For latent coordinate \((i,j)\) the identity is

\[N|N^{-1/2}p_{i,x,j}-a_{i,j}|^2=|p_{i,x,j}-\sqrt N a_{i,j}|^2.\]

For vectors \(b_i\), the parameter estimate is Jensen’s inequality \(\|N^{-1}\sum_i b_i\|^2\le N^{-1}\sum_i\|b_i\|^2\). Here \(b_i\) denotes the parameter difference between the \(i\)-th pair output and the common comparator. Its latent contribution is exactly \(N^{-1}\|p_{i,x}-\sqrt N a_i\|^2\). Thus the bound holds for any pair map, independently of convexity or proximal optimality.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Summation of the pairwise proximal energy inequalities}{Summation of the pairwise proximal energy inequalities}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ParticleEnergy-lean-2}


The averaged pairwise output need not minimize the shared-state proximal objective. The valid comparison is instead

\[
\|\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)-a\|^2
\le\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\|P(J_i z)-J_i a\|^2.
\]

Apply the single-pair energy inequality to each term. The identities \(\sum_i\|J_i(z-a)\|^2=N\|z-a\|^2\) and \(G_2=N^{-1}\sum_i g_2\circ J_i\) give

\[
\|\operatorname{Pair}_{P_\lambda}(z)-a\|^2
\le\|z-a\|^2-2\lambda\bigl(G_2(z)-G_2(a)\bigr)+\lambda^2C.
\]

This estimate uses the minimizing property only for each individual pair. It is the deterministic energy statement required in the splitting analysis; it is not by itself a stochastic convergence theorem.



A counterexample to shared-state proximal optimality has \(N=2\), \(g_2(\theta,x)=|\theta-x|\), proximal scale one, common parameter \(1\), and physical latent values \((0,4)\). The pair minimizers are \((1/2,1/2)\) and \((2,3)\), so averaging their parameter components gives \((5/4,1/2,3)\). The feasible shared-state competitor \((1,1,3)\) has objective \(3/2\), strictly below the pairwise output value \(51/32\).



To derive the valid estimate, apply the pair inequality at \(A_i z\) against \(A_i y\) and sum its \(N\) instances. The identity \(\sum_i\|A_i(z-y)\|^2=N\|z-y\|^2\) controls the initial squared distances. Divide by positive \(N\); the potential term becomes \(N^{-1}\sum_i[f(A_i z)-f(A_i y)]\). Equivalently the initial sum is \(N\|z-a\|^2\) and the potential sum is \(N[G_2(z)-G_2(a)]\), whose division by \(N>0\) gives the displayed full-state expression. Jensen supplies the parameter inequality, exact latent scaling supplies the other coordinates, and the averaged subgradient bounds supply the remainder.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Value Lipschitzness of the envelope}{Value Lipschitzness of the envelope}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingMoments-lean-1}

Lipschitz growth of the smooth gradient can now be measured from this point and integrated using concentration of the total Gibbs law.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The second moment of a bounded gradient}{The second moment of a bounded gradient}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingMoments-lean-3}

Assume \(g\) is convex, bounded below, and \(K\)-Lipschitz, with \(\lambda>0\) and \(N>0\). Proximal optimality and the value bound give the pairwise energy inequality; Jensen transfers it to the averaged particle output. The replicated-pair norm identity also proves that \(G=N^{-1}\sum_i g(J_i z)\) has the same value Lipschitz constant. Neither argument requires the averaged output to minimize a shared-state objective.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The proximal energy inequality from value Lipschitzness}{The proximal energy inequality from value Lipschitzness}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingCoupling-lean-1}


For \(p=P_\lambda(x)\), test the residual support at comparator \(a\) to bound \(f(p)-f(a)\). Apply \(f(x)-f(p)\le K\|x-p\|\) in the expansion of \(\|x-a\|^2\). Nonnegativity of \((\|x-p\|-\lambda K)^2\ge0\) gives \(\|p-a\|^2\le\|x-a\|^2-2\lambda(f(x)-f(a))+\lambda^2K^2\). Sum the pair inequalities, use Jensen for the common parameter, and divide by positive \(N\). The resulting full-state remainder is \(\lambda^2K^2\).



At the proximal point \(p\), the residual \(r=(x-p)/\lambda\) is a subgradient at \(p\). Its norm is at most \(K\) for a \(K\)-Lipschitz potential. The support at \(p\), bound \(f(x)-f(p)\le K\|x-p\|\), and squared-distance expansion give

\[\|p-y\|^2\le\|x-y\|^2-2\lambda(f(x)-f(y))+\lambda^2K^2.\]

The required replacement of output energy by input energy is exactly \(f(x)-f(p)\le K\|x-p\|\). Completion of a square bounds the remaining displacement expression by \(\lambda^2K^2\). Thus value Lipschitzness supplies the needed numerical remainder without assuming a bounded input-subgradient selection.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The averaged potential’s value Lipschitz constant}{The averaged potential’s value Lipschitz constant}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingCoupling-lean-2}


Put \(G(z)=F_g(z)/N\). Cauchy–Schwarz for the finite average and \(\sum_i\|J_i v\|^2=N\|v\|^2\) imply \(N^{-1}\sum_i\|J_i v\|\le\|v\|\). Applying the pairwise value Lipschitz estimate therefore gives

\[|G(z)-G(w)|\le\frac KN\sum_i\|J_i(z-w)\|\le K\|z-w\|.\]

The latent scaling \(N^{-1/2}\) makes the average pair squared distance equal to the state squared distance. Consequently the averaged physical-time potential \(G=F_g/N\) retains value Lipschitz constant \(K\). This bound ensures integrability of its values at square-integrable random states.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Contraction of the splitting recursion with common innovations}{Contraction of the splitting recursion with common innovations}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingContraction-lean}


Assume \(N>0\); let \(g_1\) be differentiable and \(m\)-strongly convex with \(L_1\)-Lipschitz gradient, and let \(g_2\) be convex and bounded below. Suppose \(0<m\le L_1\), \(\lambda>0\), and \(0\le\gamma\le2/(m+L_1)\). Jensen’s inequality transfers pairwise proximal nonexpansiveness to the averaged state map. In two updates driven by the same innovation, the additive perturbations cancel before proximity. The smooth gradient-step contraction therefore also bounds the full splitting update and its finite iterates.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.7]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.10]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Nonexpansiveness of the averaged pair map}{Nonexpansiveness of the averaged pair map}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingContraction-lean-1}


Jensen’s inequality for the averaged parameter difference and the exact latent squared scaling give

\[\|\operatorname{Pair}_P(z)-\operatorname{Pair}_P(w)\|^2\le N^{-1}\sum_i\|P(J_i z)-P(J_i w)\|^2.\]

If \(P\) is nonexpansive, each squared summand is at most \(\|J_i(z-w)\|^2\). Their sum is \(N\|z-w\|^2\), so the averaged map is nonexpansive.



Explicitly put \(A_i z=(\theta,\sqrt N z_i)\) and \(\Delta_i=P(A_i z)-P(A_i w)\). The parameter inequality is \(N\|\overline\Delta_\theta\|^2\le\sum_i\|\Delta_{i,\theta}\|^2\), while the latent differences are rescaled by \(N^{-1/2}\). Thus

\[\|\operatorname{Pair}P(z)-\operatorname{Pair}P(w)\|^2\le\frac1N\sum_i\|\Delta_i\|^2.\]

Nonexpansiveness of \(P\) and identity \(\sum_i\|A_i(z-w)\|^2=N\|z-w\|^2\) give the unit full-state Lipschitz bound. Positivity of \(N\) justifies division and rescaling. The argument compares two averaged outputs and does not assume that either minimizes a shared-state proximal objective.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Contraction with the same innovation}{Contraction with the same innovation}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingContraction-lean-2}


The lifted smooth potential has curvature \(Nm\) and gradient constant \(NL_1\). At step \(\gamma/N\) its gradient update contracts by \(1-m\gamma\ge0\). For the same innovation the noisy forward difference is this deterministic difference. Composition with the nonexpansive pairwise proximal map yields

\[\|T_\lambda(z,\xi)-T_\lambda(w,\xi)\|\le(1-m\gamma)\|z-w\|.\]

Induction gives factor \((1-m\gamma)^n\). Writing \(q=1-m\gamma\), the step restriction ensures \(q\ge0\). Starting from \(e_0=\|z-w\|\), the induction step is \(e_{k+1}\le q e_k\le q^{k+1}e_0\). Equivalently, with \(q=1-m\gamma\) the discrepancies satisfy \(e_{k+1}\le q e_k\) and are bounded at time \(n\) by \(q^n e_0\).



Only equality of the innovations is used, so this stability statement holds for deterministic sequences as well as Gaussian ones. Convergence to a Gibbs law additionally requires a stationary reference and a local-error estimate.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Integrated quadratic and convex-energy estimates}{Integrated quadratic and convex-energy estimates}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingL2Energy-lean}


Apply the deterministic energy estimate to \(L^2\) random vectors. Lipschitz growth makes the scalar potential values integrable, and square integrability makes all squared-distance terms integrable. Weighted Young inequalities control the smooth local defect without losing the squared contraction. Integrated convex supports retain the perturbation-force pairing in a form that can be combined with the expected potential difference.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Weighted Young inequality and squared contraction}{Weighted Young inequality and squared contraction}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingL2Energy-lean-1}


For \(t>0\), expansion and \((t\|v\|-\|r\|)^2\ge0\) give

\[\|v+r\|^2\le(1+t)\|v\|^2+(1+t^{-1})\|r\|^2.\]

If \(0<t\le1\) and \(\|v\|\le(1-t)\|e\|\), the inequality \((1+t)(1-t)^2\le1-t\) leaves a squared contraction with coefficient one minus the parameter. The identity \(\|v+r+b\|^2=\|v+r\|^2+2\langle v+r+b,b\rangle-\|b\|^2\) separately retains the force cross term.



Choosing the Young parameter as \(mh\) gives \((1+mh)(1-mh)^2\le1-mh\) under \(mh\le1\), while multiplying the squared local defect by \(1+(mh)^{-1}\). Keeping the integrated perturbation force in its inner product permits its convex-energy contribution to combine with the proximal energy difference, rather than estimating each term by an unrelated norm bound.



\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Integrability of potential and quadratic terms}{Integrability of potential and quadratic terms}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingL2Energy-lean-2}


For globally \(K\)-Lipschitz scalar function \(f\), subtract \(f(0)\). The centered growth is at most \(K\|X\|\), so \(f(X)\in L^2\subset L^1\) on a probability space. The \(L^2\) norm satisfies \(\|X\|_2^2=\int\|X(\omega)\|^2dP\).



For observable \(f\), the direct bound is \(|f(X)|\le K\|X\|+|f(0)|\). Thus an \(L^2\) state gives \(f(X)\in L^2\subset L^1\). Centering \(f\) justifies the composition estimate. The \(L^2\) inner product and identity \(\langle X,X\rangle=\|X\|^2\) give \(\|X\|_{L^2}^2=\int\|X(\omega)\|^2dP\). More generally, bound \(|f(x)|\le|f(0)|+K\|x\|\) makes composition with an \(L^2\) state an \(L^2\) function. Squared differences of \(L^2\) states are also integrable. Hence the potential and quadratic expectations below are finite, and linearity may be applied without subtraction of infinite quantities.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The integrated proximal energy inequality}{The integrated proximal energy inequality}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingL2Energy-lean-3}


Assume \(\|T(x)-y\|^2\le\|x-y\|^2-2h(f(x)-f(y))+C\) and Lipschitz maps \(T,f\). Substitute \(S,Y\in L^2\) pointwise on a probability-one representative event. The Lipschitz composition represents \(T(S)\) almost surely. Each term is integrable by the preceding growth and moment bounds. Integral monotonicity and linearity therefore give

\[\|T(S)-Y\|_2^2\le\|S-Y\|_2^2-2h(\mathbb E f(S)-\mathbb E f(Y))+C.\]

This is the same energy inequality in \(L^2\). The additive constant is unchanged because the source has mass one. The statement concerns the random output of the actual composed map, as identified by its almost-sure representative.





Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.13.2]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.13.3]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.11.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Integrated convex support and the terminal-state difference}{Integrated convex support and the terminal-state difference}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingL2Energy-lean-4}


The support inequality for a differentiable convex perturbation gives \(\langle S-Y,\nabla e(Y)\rangle\le e(S)-e(Y)\) pointwise. Integrability permits the same inequality for the \(L^2\) inner product. For a third vector \(Z\), identity \(S-Z=(S-Y)+(Y-Z)\) leaves additional pairing \(\langle Y-Z,\nabla e(Y)\rangle_{L^2}\).



Explicitly, for convex differentiable \(e\) the inequality is \(\langle S-Y,\nabla e(Y)\rangle\le e(S)-e(Y)\). The gradient composition and state difference belong to \(L^2\) under the stated bounds, so Cauchy–Schwarz makes their pairing integrable. Integration consequently replaces the reference-force pairing by an expected potential difference, while retaining the endpoint-difference pairing whose future-noise part is shown to vanish separately.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Local smooth drift defect and the future cross term}{Local smooth drift defect and the future cross term}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingProcess-lean}


Suppose the continuous Hilbert-valued reference satisfies \(Y_t-Y_0=-\int_0^t g(Y_s)ds+B_t\) with \(\|g(Y_t)\|\le G\) and \(\|B_t\|\le\sigma\sqrt t\). Its increments are bounded by the sum of a term linear in time and a term proportional to the square root of time. The smooth drift’s Lipschitz constant then bounds its integrated local defect. For a bounded perturbation observable, first use orthogonality of the future noise; the remaining drift pairing is linear in the remaining time.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The integrated smooth drift defect}{The integrated smooth drift defect}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingProcess-lean-1}


The integral equation and the integral norm inequality give \(\|Y_t-Y_0\|\le Gt+\sigma\sqrt t\). If \(a\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz, then for \(0\le t\le h\), \(\|a(Y_t)-a(Y_0)\|\le L(Gh+\sigma\sqrt h)\). Integration over length \(h\) therefore yields

\[\left\|\int_0^h(a(Y_t)-a(Y_0))dt\right\|\le Lh(Gh+\sigma\sqrt h).\]

Equivalently, for solution \(Y_t-Y_0=-\int_0^t g(Y_s)ds+B_t\) with \(\|g(Y_s)\|\le G\) and \(\|B_t\|\le\nu\sqrt t\), one has \(\|Y_t-Y_0\|\le tG+\nu\sqrt t\). For smooth component \(a\) with Lipschitz constant \(L\), this gives

\[\left\|\int_0^h(a(Y_t)-a(Y_0))dt\right\|\le Lh(Gh+\nu\sqrt h).\]

The estimate replaces both time factors by their endpoint maxima on \(0\le t\le h\), using monotonicity of \(t\) and \(\sqrt t\), and multiplies by interval length. Thus from \(\|Y_t-Y_0\|\le Gt+\sigma\sqrt t\), multiplication by \(L\) controls the smooth force difference; for \(t\in[0,h]\) the factors \(t\) and \(\sqrt t\) are bounded by their endpoint values. Regularity may require Lipschitz continuity of the full drift, but only the smooth component’s constant enters this numerical bound.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.11.1]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.11.6]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The future-drift pairing after noise orthogonality}{The future-drift pairing after noise orthogonality}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingProcess-lean-2}


Subtract the reference equations at \(t,h\) to obtain

\[Y_t-Y_h=\int_t^h g(Y_s)ds-(B_h-B_t).\]

If \(\|b\|\le K\) and \(\langle b,B_h-B_t\rangle=0\), the noise pairing is zero. Cauchy–Schwarz on the remaining drift integral gives \(\langle Y_t-Y_h,b\rangle\le(h-t)GK\), avoiding an order-\(\sqrt{h-t}\) bound.



Splitting the integral at \(t\) gives the same difference identity

\[Y_t-Y_h=\int_t^h g(Y_s)ds-(B_h-B_t).\]

Pairing with \(b\) and applying orthogonality gives

\[\langle Y_t-Y_h,b\rangle\le\left\|\int_t^h g(Y_s)ds\right\|\|b\|\le(h-t)GK.\]

In the application the inner product is in \(L^2\), so this cancellation is of the expected pairing. Subtraction at \(t\) and \(h\) leaves future drift integral of norm at most \((h-t)G\), hence remainder \((h-t)GK\). Brownian sample increments themselves do not vanish.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Independence under grouping of random variables}{Independence under grouping of random variables}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-IndependenceHelpers-lean}


Independent source variables may be grouped into an initialized past and a future increment by factoring their joint distributions and reassociating product measures. The results below use joint independence of the specified blocks. Pairwise independence of three separate variables would not imply the required independence of one variable from a pair.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{An independent family with two indices}{An independent family with two indices}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-IndependenceHelpers-lean-1}


Assume \(X\perp Y\) and define \(Z_{\rm false}=X\) and \(Z_{\rm true}=Y\). To verify independence of this two-element family, consider its finite index subsets. The empty subset uses probability mass one, and each singleton gives an identity. For the subset of both indices the condition is exactly \(P(X\in A,Y\in B)=P(X\in A)P(Y\in B)\).



Thus two-variable independence is equivalent to the factorization required for the corresponding two-element independent family. No additional independence condition is introduced by this indexing.



\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Reassociation of a jointly independent triple}{Reassociation of a jointly independent triple}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-IndependenceHelpers-lean-2}


Assume measurable variables satisfy \(X\perp(Y,Z)\) and \(Y\perp Z\). Their triple law factors as \(\mu_X\otimes(\mu_Y\otimes\mu_Z)\). Product reassociation gives \((\mu_X\otimes\mu_Y)\otimes\mu_Z\), while projection gives \(\mu_{(X,Y)}=\mu_X\otimes\mu_Y\). Hence \((X,Y)\perp Z\).



Indeed \(X\) is independent of the entire pair \((Y,Z)\), and \(Y\) and \(Z\) are independent. These hypotheses give \(\mu_X\otimes(\mu_Y\otimes\mu_Z)\), whose reassociation is \((\mu_X\otimes\mu_Y)\otimes\mu_Z\). This proves independence of \((X,Y)\) and \(Z\). The first two product marginals are the joint law of \((X,Y)\), so the conclusion is \((X,Y)\perp Z\). Equality of measurable pushforward measures proves the statement also for path-valued variables. The hypotheses are stronger than mere pairwise independence.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Orthogonality of future Brownian increments to past observables}{Orthogonality of future Brownian increments to past observables}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOrthogonality-lean}


Assume independent full scalar Brownian paths with measurable evaluations and initialization independent of the entire Brownian family. The integral solution is measurable with respect to initialization and the stopped noise path. A future Brownian increment is independent of this past and centered. Its expected inner product with any square-integrable Lipschitz observable of the reference state is therefore zero. This is an expectation identity, not a samplewise orthogonality claim.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.54]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.25]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.31]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Independence of vector stopped past and shifted future}{Independence of vector stopped past and shifted future}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOrthogonality-lean-1}


For each scalar coordinate, the shifted future \(B_i(T+s)-B_i(T)\) is independent of the stopped past \(B_i(\min(t,T))\). Independence of the full coordinate paths gives joint independence across these coordinatewise pairs. Selecting and grouping the disjoint past and future indices therefore gives independence of the entire vector-valued past and future path families.



The stopped path records all coordinate values up to time \(t\); the shifted future records increments after \(t\). The resulting independence is between the sigma-algebras generated by these full path blocks. Consequently any measurable function of the entire past is independent of every measurable future increment. This uses full-path independence and Brownian independent increments, rather than unrelated pairwise independence assertions.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The pointwise Brownian vector and its square-integrable representative}{The pointwise Brownian vector and its square-integrable representative}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOrthogonality-lean-2}


Define the pointwise vector noise by \(\mathcal B_t=\sqrt2(B(t_+)-B(0))\) with \(t_+=\max(t,0)\). The constructed \(L^2\) Brownian noise agrees almost surely with this expression. Each scalar evaluation is measurable, and finite vector assembly, subtraction, and scaling preserve measurability. Its Gaussian coordinate second moments prove square integrability.



The \(L^2\) class and the pointwise vector thus have the same laws and integrals. Independence and centering may be transferred by their almost-sure equality. The nonnegative-time definition specifies the domain of Brownian evaluation and does not change the positive-time process.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Independence of the initialized past from future increments}{Independence of the initialized past from future increments}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOrthogonality-lean-3}


The stopped Brownian path is independent of the future increment. Initialization is independent of their joint pair. Reassociation of the product law therefore makes the pair consisting of initialization and stopped past independent of the future increment. Any variable measurable with respect to that initialized past inherits the same independence.



A variable only almost everywhere strongly measurable for the past admits a measurable representative with the same law. Almost-sure equality then transfers the conclusion to its \(L^2\) representative. The grouping relation is \((\text{initial},\text{past})\mid\text{future}\). Thus independence for the later reference observable follows from its past measurability and the input assumptions; it is not added as an independent assertion about the solution.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Expected orthogonality from independence and centering}{Expected orthogonality from independence and centering}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOrthogonality-lean-4}


Scalar Brownian centering and commutation of finite coordinate projections with integration give \(\mathbb E B_t=0\), and subtraction and scaling give \(\mathbb E\mathcal B_t=0\). For independent \(L^2\) vectors \(X,W\), Cauchy–Schwarz proves integrability of the inner product. Independence and bilinear integration give \(\langle X,W\rangle_{L^2}=\langle\mathbb E X,\mathbb E W\rangle\).



When \(W\) is centered, the latter inner product of means is zero. Thus no norm bound on the other mean is needed: independence and centering give exact expected orthogonality.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Orthogonality for observables of the adapted integral solution}{Orthogonality for observables of the adapted integral solution}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOrthogonality-lean-5}


The integral-equation construction makes \(Y_t\) measurable for the initialized Brownian past. A Lipschitz observable \(b(Y_t)\) remains measurable for that past and belongs to \(L^2\). Independence of future increments and their zero mean imply

\[\langle b(Y_t),\mathcal B_h-\mathcal B_t\rangle_{L^2}=0,\qquad0\le t\le h.\]

For observable \(b(Y_t)\), both factors are in \(L^2\), so their pairing is integrable and

\[\mathbb E\langle b(Y_t),B_h-B_t\rangle=\left\langle\mathbb E b(Y_t),\mathbb E(B_h-B_t)\right\rangle=0.\]

The \(L^2\) inner product is this expectation. The state at time \(t\) depends only on initialization and noise up to \(t\). Composing with the drift observable preserves this dependence, while the increment over \([t,h]\) is independent and centered. This identity removes the contribution that an absolute norm bound would estimate at order \(\sqrt{h-t}\) in the subsequent energy comparison.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Expected energy comparison for the perturbation force}{Expected energy comparison for the perturbation force}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingCrossEnergy-lean}


Let \(e\) be convex and differentiable, assume \(e\le f\le e+\delta\), and let \(Y_t\) have the stated stationary smoothed law, integral equation, and future-noise orthogonality. Assume the value Lipschitz bounds needed for integrability. Convex support bounds the force pairing by an envelope-energy difference. Stationarity identifies the time-dependent reference energy with the terminal energy, and the uniform gap then compares this with the original nonsmooth energy in the proximal estimate.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The expected uniform value-gap inequality}{The expected uniform value-gap inequality}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingCrossEnergy-lean-1}


Suppose \(f(x)\le e(x)+\delta\) and both functions are Lipschitz. For \(L^2\) variable \(X\) their values are integrable, so integral monotonicity gives \(\mathbb E f(X)-\mathbb E e(X)\le\delta\). In the notation \(f\le e+\delta\) and an \(L^2\) state, the mass-one calculation is \(\mathbb Ef(X)\le\mathbb Ee(X)+\delta P(\Omega)=\mathbb Ee(X)+\delta\).



The subsequent comparison uses \(e\le f\) at the algorithm’s intermediate state and \(f\le e+\delta\) at the reference endpoint. The integrated gap is \(\delta\) because the source is a probability. All expected values are finite before subtraction, and no derivative of the original nonsmooth function is used.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The stationary convex-support and future-drift estimates}{The stationary convex-support and future-drift estimates}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingCrossEnergy-lean-2}


Convex support bounds \(\langle S-Y_h,\nabla e(Y_t)\rangle\) by \(\mathbb E e(S)-\mathbb E e(Y_t)+\langle Y_t-Y_h,\nabla e(Y_t)\rangle\). Stationarity gives equality of \(\mathbb E e(Y_t)\) and \(\mathbb E e(Y_h)\). The future-noise estimate bounds the remaining drift pairing by \(hGK\). The gap \(e\le f\le e+\delta\) changes the difference of the two energies by at most \(\delta\). Integration therefore gives

\[2\left\langle S-Y_h,\int_0^h\nabla e(Y_t)dt\right\rangle-2h(\mathbb E f(S)-\mathbb E f(Y_h))\le2h\delta+2h^2GK.\]

More explicitly, for \(b_t=\nabla e(Y_t)\) in \(L^2\) the support and future-increment bounds give

\[\langle S-Y_h,b_t\rangle\le\mathbb Ee(S)-\mathbb Ee(Y_t)+(h-t)GK.\]

Stationarity replaces \(\mathbb Ee(Y_t)\) by \(\mathbb Ee(Y_h)\). Integration over \([0,h]\) is legitimate because \(b_t\) is continuous and the fixed inner-product functional commutes with the Bochner integral. A valid upper bound is \(h[\mathbb Ee(S)-\mathbb Ee(Y_h)]+h^2GK\).



The decomposition \(S-Y_h=(S-Y_t)+(Y_t-Y_h)\) separates the convex support term from the future-increment term. Again \(\mathbb E e(Y_t)\) equals \(\mathbb E e(Y_h)\). At \(S\) use \(e\le f\), and at \(Y_h\) use \(f\le e+\delta\). Only one additive gap \(\delta\) remains. This is the energy comparison used to cancel the original potential difference in the splitting estimate.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The synchronous one-step splitting estimate}{The synchronous one-step splitting estimate}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOneStep-lean}


In the Hilbert space \(L^2\), suppose \(f_1\) is \(m\)-strongly convex with \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient. Let a Lipschitz map \(T\) satisfy the proximal energy inequality for \(f\). Assume the stationary reference satisfies the integral equation for \(f_1+e\) and \(e\le f\le e+\delta\), together with the stated moment and orthogonality conditions. Subtraction from the numerical forward step decomposes the discrepancy into a contractive smooth difference, a local smooth defect, and an integrated envelope force. The energy comparison and weighted Young inequality then give the one-step squared-error bound.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The four local forcing terms}{The four local forcing terms}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOneStep-lean-1}


For \(m,h>0\), drift bound \(G\), noise coefficient \(\sigma\), perturbation-gradient bound \(K\), and potential gap \(\delta\), set

\[\mathcal F=(1+(mh)^{-1})[Lh(Gh+\sigma\sqrt h)]^2+2h\delta+2h^2GK+h^2K^2.\]

The first term is the squared smooth local defect with Young factor \(1+(mh)^{-1}\). The other terms are, respectively, the finite potential-gap contribution, the reference drift paired with the bounded perturbation gradient, and the proximal quadratic remainder. These distinct estimates will bound the sum by a constant times the squared step.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The error decomposition and energy comparison}{The error decomposition and energy comparison}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingOneStep-lean-2}


Define \(S=X-h\nabla f_1(X)+B_h\), \(v=(X-h\nabla f_1(X))-(Y_0-h\nabla f_1(Y_0))\), \(R=\int_0^h[\nabla f_1(Y_t)-\nabla f_1(Y_0)]dt\), and \(H=\int_0^h\nabla e(Y_t)dt\). The shared noise and reference integral equation give \(S-Y_h=v+R+H\). Expand its square and integrate the energy inequality for \(T(S)\). The expected \(f\) difference cancels with the corresponding force pairing up to the cross-energy bound. Smooth-step contraction and weighted Young control \(\|v+R\|^2\), while the local estimate controls \(R\). Discarding \(-\|H\|^2\le0\) gives

\[\|T(S)-Y_h\|_2^2\le(1-mh)\|X-Y_0\|_2^2+\mathcal F.\]

The conditions \(m\le L\) and \(h\le2/(m+L)\) imply \(mh\le1\), as required in the weighted contraction estimate. In the shorter notation \(a=\nabla f_1\), \(b=\nabla e\), \(S=X-ha(X)+B_h\), \(v=[X-ha(X)]-[Y_0-ha(Y_0)]\), \(R=\int_0^h[a(Y_t)-a(Y_0)]dt\), and \(H=\int_0^h b(Y_t)dt\), the exact difference is \(S-Y_h=v+R+H\) and its squared rearrangement is

\[\|S-Y_h\|^2=\|v+R\|^2+2\langle S-Y_h,H\rangle-\|H\|^2.\]

This identity retains the integrated envelope force until its energy comparison is applied. The nonpositive square can then be dropped. Weighted Young leaves coefficient \(1-mh\) on the preceding squared error. The forcing is derived from these inequalities rather than assumed as a stochastic recurrence; the Gaussian reference construction supplies the stated reference premises.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The greatest-lower-bound argument}{The greatest-lower-bound argument}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingTransport-lean-1}


If \(q=0\), choose one finite-cost input coupling. If the output square is at most \(c\), nonnegativity of the other term already proves the claim. Otherwise the coefficient is positive and one may set \(r=\sqrt{(W_2(\mu',\nu')^2-c)/q}\). Each finite coupling estimate implies \(r\le\operatorname{cost}(p)\), and infinite-cost couplings satisfy the same extended lower bound. Hence \(r\le\inf_p\operatorname{cost}(p)=W_2(\mu,\nu)\). \[W_2(\mu',\nu')^2\le qW_2(\mu,\nu)^2+c.\]

In the notation \(A=W_2(\mu',\nu')^2\), the hypothesis is \(A\le qC^2+c\) for all finite costs. The case \(q=0\) gives \(A\le c\) directly. The case \(A\le c\) follows using \(q\ge0\). Otherwise \(r=\sqrt{(A-c)/q}\) is real and positive, and all coupling costs are at least \(r\).



Equivalently, costs may approach the finite infimum from above; continuity of squaring makes their squared costs tend to \(W_2(\mu,\nu)^2\). The fixed output bound survives that limit. Equivalently multiplication by \(N\) gives lifted value constant \(NK\). The envelope’s single-pair constant \(K\) yields \(\|\nabla V_{N,\eta}^{(2)}\|\le NK\). This uses the norm bound for the derivative of a Lipschitz function, rather than the gradient Lipschitz constant \(N/\eta\), and is consequently uniform as \(\eta\downarrow0\). \subsection{\texorpdfstring{The summed envelope gap and split-potential identity}{The summed envelope gap and split-potential identity}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-ParticleSmoothing-lean-2}

\section{\texorpdfstring{A finite coefficient for the splitting error floor}{A finite coefficient for the splitting error floor}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-SplittingRate-lean}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The explicit nonnegative coefficient}{The explicit nonnegative coefficient}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingRate-lean-1}


For \(m>0\) and \(G,K\ge0\) define

\[C(m,L,G,\sigma,K)=\frac{4L^2}{m^2}\left(\frac{G^2}{m}+\sigma^2\right)+\frac2m(GK+K^2).\]

Every squared term is nonnegative and the denominators are positive, giving \(C\ge0\). Each summand of \(C\) is finite under \(m>0\) and the stated nonnegative magnitude bounds. \subsection{\texorpdfstring{The quadratic-step bound on local forcing}{The quadratic-step bound on local forcing}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-SplittingRate-lean-2}

The use of \((a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2\) separates drift and noise. Under \(h\le1/m\) the additional powers of \(h\) are bounded by curvature constants, leaving a coefficient times \(h^2\). \section{\texorpdfstring{Wasserstein regularity of the integrated parameter force}{Wasserstein regularity of the integrated parameter force}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldDrift-lean}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Expectation differences for a Lipschitz observable}{Expectation differences for a Lipschitz observable}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldDrift-lean-1}

\[\left\|\int\phi\,d\mu-\int\phi\,d\nu\right\|\le K\left(\int\|x-y\|^2dc\right)^{1/2}.\]

The infimum of coupling costs gives \(K W_2(\mu,\nu)\). The coupling integral identity is

\[\int\varphi\,d\mu-\int\varphi\,d\nu=\int[\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)]\,dc(x,y).\]

Integrability includes the observable’s value at zero. Its Lipschitz constant bounds the integrand difference by the pair distance, and \(\|\cdot\|_1\le\|\cdot\|_2\) bounds its first moment by its quadratic cost. The input second moments make the infimum finite; no optimal coupling is required.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Separate parameter and probability-law perturbations}{Separate parameter and probability-law perturbations}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldDrift-lean-4}

\]

\|b_V(\theta,\mu)-b_V(\theta',\nu)\|

\section{\texorpdfstring{Exact iid variance and the empirical parameter force}{Exact iid variance and the empirical parameter force}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldVariance-lean}


For \(N>0\), independent centered square-integrable Hilbert-valued vectors have zero mixed expected inner products. Expanding the finite squared sum and integrating termwise therefore gives an exact \(1/N\) variance factor for their sample mean. The finite product measure supplies the required independence. Applying the identity to the parameter-gradient observable gives the empirical mean-field force fluctuation.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The centered off-diagonal expectations}{The centered off-diagonal expectations}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldVariance-lean-1}


View the supplied \(L^2\) functions as \(L^2\) vectors. Cauchy–Schwarz makes each pairwise inner product integrable, so finite expansion gives

\[\mathbb E\left\|\sum_iX_i\right\|^2=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb E\langle X_i,X_j\rangle.\]

For \(i\ne j\), independence and zero means give \(\langle\mathbb E X_i,\mathbb E X_j\rangle=0\). The diagonal terms are \(\mathbb E\|X_i\|^2\). For distinct indices \(i\ne j\), centering alone would not remove covariance; independence is required for this factorization. Products of \(L^2\) norms are integrable, which justifies the complete termwise identity.





Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.11.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The variance of an independent sample mean}{The variance of an independent sample mean}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldVariance-lean-2}


Let \(N>0\) and let \(\phi\) be square-integrable under probability \(\mu\). On \(\mu^{\otimes N}\) the coordinate samples are independent. Center \(\phi\) by its expectation, expand the squared sample mean, and cancel its off-diagonal pairings. This gives

\[
\mathbb E_{\mu^{\otimes N}}
 \left\|\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\phi(X_i)-\mathbb E_\mu\phi\right\|^2
 =\frac1N\mathbb E_\mu\|\phi-\mathbb E_\mu\phi\|^2.
\]

The root mean square sampling factor is \(N^{-1/2}\). This variance identity is an ingredient in particle comparison, not by itself a propagation-of-chaos theorem. Exchangeability alone would not cancel covariances.



Indeed let \(C_i=\phi(X_i)-\mathbb E\phi\). Its average is \(N^{-1}\sum_iC_i\), the expected squared sum is \(N\mathbb E\|C_1\|^2\), and the averaging prefactor is \(N^{-2}\). Their product is \(N^{-1}\mathbb E\|C_1\|^2\). Taking square roots gives \(1/\sqrt N\). Independence here comes from the finite product construction, rather than an assumption about interacting particle states.



\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The empirical parameter-gradient variance}{The empirical parameter-gradient variance}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldVariance-lean-3}


Assume \(N>0\), fix \(\theta\), and put \(\phi(x)=\nabla_\theta U(\theta,x)\). Joint-gradient Lipschitzness gives affine growth of this observable in its latent input, so it is square-integrable under every finite-second-moment law \(\mu\). Its expectation is \(b_U(\theta,\mu)\). The independent-sample identity therefore gives

\[\mathbb E\left\|\frac1N\sum_i\nabla_\theta U(\theta,X_i)-b_U(\theta,\mu)\right\|^2=\frac1N\mathbb E\|\nabla_\theta U(\theta,X)-b_U(\theta,\mu)\|^2.\]

This is the exact variance of the empirical parameter force. Its finite variance is derived from the gradient and latent-moment assumptions rather than postulated as an additional sampling-error bound.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The particle drift on a product of square-integrable coordinate spaces}{The particle drift on a product of square-integrable coordinate spaces}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldParticleDrift-lean}


Use a finite product of coordinate \(L^2\) spaces to describe the physical particle system. Specifically let \(H_N=L^2(P;\Theta)\times\prod_{i<N}L^2(P;X)\) with \(\|z\|=\max(\|z_\theta\|_2,\max_i\|z_i\|_2)\). Reconstruct a joint pair from the common parameter and each latent coordinate, then evaluate its gradient. Averaging the parameter forces and retaining each latent force gives a Lipschitz drift with constant independent of particle count. The maximum norm directly bounds each coordinate without exchangeability assumptions.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Coordinate inclusions and square-integrable contractions}{Coordinate inclusions and square-integrable contractions}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldParticleDrift-lean-1}


Define the latent inclusion \(I_x(x)=(0,x)\). Adding zero parameter coordinates gives \(\|I_xx\|=\|x\|\), and latent projection has norm at most one. For a pointwise linear contraction \(C\), integration of \(\|C(X)\|^2\le\|X\|^2\) gives \(\|C\circ X\|_2\le\|X\|_2\).



Thus coordinate inclusion and projection are contractions also in \(L^2\). They introduce no dimension factor in reconstruction of the random pair. The maximum product norm consequently controls every parameter and tagged latent-coordinate difference.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.3.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.9.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Reconstruction of a random joint pair}{Reconstruction of a random joint pair}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldParticleDrift-lean-2}


Set \(J(\Theta,X)=I_\theta\Theta+I_xX\). Its representative is \((\Theta(\omega),X(\omega))\) almost surely. The bounded linear inclusions and triangle inequality give

\[\|J(\Theta,X)-J(\Theta',X')\|_2\le\|\Theta-\Theta'\|_2+\|X-X'\|_2.\]

The coordinate \(L^2\) differences bound the reconstructed pair difference. Its representative is exactly \((\Theta(\omega),X(\omega))\), so evaluation of a joint potential or gradient on this Hilbert-space pair agrees with evaluation at the intended parameter and latent vectors.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.3.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The empirical particle drift}{The empirical particle drift}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldParticleDrift-lean-3}


Apply \(\nabla V\) to the reconstructed pair \(J(\Theta,X_i)\) for each particle. Define the empirical parameter drift and the individual latent drifts by

\[\mathcal A_N(z)=\left(\frac1N\sum_i\nabla_\theta V(\Theta,X_i),\ (\nabla_xV(\Theta,X_i))_i\right).\]

The representative formulas follow from composition and the finite-sum identity. Only the parameter components are averaged, with division justified by \(N>0\). This is the interacting physical-particle drift, retaining the common random parameter; no mean-field expectation is substituted in this definition.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Particle-count-independent Lipschitzness and the integral solution}{Particle-count-independent Lipschitzness and the integral solution}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldParticleDrift-lean-4}


Every coordinate difference is bounded by the maximum product distance, so pair reconstruction and gradient regularity give \(\|\nabla V(J_i z)-\nabla V(J_i w)\|_2\le2L\|z-w\|\). Averaging the \(N\) parameter terms and dividing by \(N>0\) preserves this bound. The maximum latent-coordinate norm preserves it as well. Hence \(\operatorname{Lip}(\mathcal A_N)\le2L\) independently of \(N\). Picard gives continuous \(Z\) satisfying \(Z_t-Z_0=-\int_0^t\mathcal A_N(Z_s)ds+B_t\) for continuous input with \(B_0=0\).



The state consists of one parameter \(L^2\) vector and finitely many latent \(L^2\) vectors. For particle \(i\), reconstruction gives \(\|\operatorname{pair}(\theta,x_i)-\operatorname{pair}(\vartheta,y_i)\|_2\le\|\theta-\vartheta\|_2+\|x_i-y_i\|_2\le2\|z-w\|\). The \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient therefore bounds its force difference by \(2L\|z-w\|\). Averaging cancels factor \(N\) and gives constant \(2L\). Equivalently the reconstruction factor two is multiplied by \(L\); averaging \(N\) parameter bounds and taking the maximum of latent bounds leaves \(2L\). The finite-horizon integral equation follows from completeness and global drift Lipschitzness.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Independent copies and the empirical parameter-force residual}{Independent copies and the empirical parameter-force residual}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldCopies-lean}


Independent copies of the mean-field state give an empirical drift residual with exact \(N^{-1/2}\) norm factor. Starting with a joint random state on \(Q\), use the finite product \(P=Q^{\otimes N}\) and its coordinate pullbacks. When the reference parameter is deterministic, all copies have the same parameter while their latent components are independent. The copy maps are bounded and linear, so they commute with the reference integral equation. All latent drift residuals cancel; only the centered empirical parameter force remains.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The measure-preserving coordinate copy map}{The measure-preserving coordinate copy map}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldCopies-lean-1}


For product coordinate \(\omega_i\) define \(C_iX=X\circ\omega_i\). Evaluation preserves the reference measure, so pullback is an \(L^2\) isometry and a bounded linear map. Its representative equals \(X(\omega_i)\) almost surely. Coordinate substitution gives \(C_i(CX)=C(C_iX)\) for bounded linear \(C\), and constants satisfy \(C_i(\operatorname{const}x)=\operatorname{const}x\).



The evaluation \(\omega\mapsto\omega_i\) has pushforward \(Q\); hence integration of \(\|X(\omega_i)\|^2\) gives the original second moment. Pullback is consequently an isometry on \(L^2\). These pointwise and integral identities justify copying the projected reference equations.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The centered empirical fluctuation norm}{The centered empirical fluctuation norm}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldCopies-lean-2}


For \(N>0\) define \(\mathcal C X=X-\operatorname{const}(\mathbb EX)\) and \(\Delta_N(X)=N^{-1}\sum_iC_iX-\operatorname{const}(\mathbb EX)\). Finite-sum representative identities identify the latter with the centered sample mean. Independence on the product space gives

\[\|\Delta_N(X)\|_{L^2(P)}^2=\frac1N\|\mathcal C X\|_{L^2(Q)}^2,\qquad\|\Delta_N(X)\|_2=\frac{\|\mathcal C X\|_2}{\sqrt N}.\]

The pullback \(X\mapsto X(\omega_i)\) preserves \(Q\) and is a linear isometry. Thus the centered copy sum is an \(L^2\) vector represented by that sample mean. The exact variance statement is

\[\|\text{empirical fluctuation}\|_2^2=\frac{\|X-\mathbb EX\|_2^2}{N},\]

Taking nonnegative square roots gives factor \(N^{-1/2}\). The centered force norm is divided by \(\sqrt N\). This uses independence of reference copies; the later interacting particle states are not assumed independent.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The reference particle state with deterministic parameter}{The reference particle state with deterministic parameter}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldCopies-lean-3}


Define the reference particle state by

\[\mathcal R_N(X)=\left(\operatorname{const}(\mathbb E P_\theta X),\ (P_xC_iX)_i\right).\]

Bounded linear maps commute with expectation, and a constant has its original value as expectation under a probability measure. Thus if \(P_\theta X=\operatorname{const}\theta\), the parameter component of \(\mathcal R_N(X)\) is \(\operatorname{const}\theta\).



The latent coordinates are independent coordinate copies, while the deterministic parameter is unchanged. The construction is a bounded linear map, which permits passage through the reference time integral.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}; \cite[Section~3.11.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Reconstruction and drift composition for copied pairs}{Reconstruction and drift composition for copied pairs}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldCopies-lean-4}


Recombining the parameter and latent projections recovers the original \(L^2\) vector. For deterministic parameter this gives \(J_i\mathcal R_N(X)=C_iX\). Composition of the copied state with a Lipschitz pointwise drift is the same as copying the composition, since both representatives equal \(b(X(\omega_i))\) almost surely.



For the gradient, this common representative is \(\nabla V(X(\omega_i))\). Hence each reconstructed reference pair has precisely the copied joint drift. This equality causes the latent components to cancel in the particle-minus-reference drift.




Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.13.2]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.13.3]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}; \cite[Section~3.1.1]{LangevinLean2026IPLA}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The empirical parameter-force drift residual}{The empirical parameter-force drift residual}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldCopies-lean-5}


Apply the particle drift to \(\mathcal R_N(X)\) and compare it with \(\mathcal R_N(\mathcal A_V(X))\). Reconstruction and gradient commutation identify all latent components, leaving

\[\mathcal A_N(\mathcal R_NX)-\mathcal R_N(\mathcal A_VX)=\left(\Delta_N(P_\theta\nabla V(X)),0\right).\]

The maximum product norm and sample-mean variance identity give residual norm \(\|\mathcal C(P_\theta\nabla V(X))\|_2/\sqrt N\). In detail, latent coordinate \(i\) combines with the deterministic parameter to recover joint copy \(i\). The parameter discrepancy is

\[\frac1N\sum_i\nabla_\theta V(\theta,X_i)-\mathbb E\nabla_\theta V(\theta,X).\]

It is the empirical parameter force minus its expectation. Its norm is the centered single-copy force norm divided by \(\sqrt N\), equivalently \(\|\mathcal C(P_\theta\nabla V(X))\|_2/\sqrt N\). This exact residual supplies the sampling forcing used in the comparison theorem.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Grönwall comparison with an extra continuous noise input}{Grönwall comparison with an extra continuous noise input}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldForcing-lean}


A common sampling factor gives an \(N^{-1/2}\) comparison for an integral drift defect and an additional continuous input. In a Banach space let \(F\) be \(L\)-Lipschitz. Compare two integral equations with common input \(B\), additional input \(A\) in one equation, and reference drift \(G\). Subtracting the additional input before applying Grönwall yields a differentiable remainder without differentiating either noise path.



Earlier results used here: \cite[Section~3.43]{LangevinLean2026ULASC}.
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The difference after subtraction of the additional input}{The difference after subtraction of the additional input}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldForcing-lean-1}


Let \(L\ge0\) and \(T\ge0\), and define \(\Psi(L,T)=\int_0^T e^{L(T-s)}ds\). Thus \(\Psi(0,T)=T\), and \(\Psi(L,T)=(e^{LT}-1)/L\) for \(L>0\). Assume \(\|A_t\|\le a\), \(\|F(Y_t)-G_t\|\le C\), and

\[X_t-x=-\int_0^tF(X_s)ds+B_t+A_t,\qquad Y_t-x=-\int_0^tG_sds+B_t.\]

Put \(D_t=X_t-Y_t-A_t\). Subtraction cancels the common input, and the Lipschitz and defect bounds give \(\|D_t\|\le\int_0^t[L\|D_s\|+La+C]ds\). Grönwall then yields \(\|X_t-Y_t\|\le(C+La)\Psi(L,T)+a\) on \([0,T]\).



The continuous inputs \(B,A\) may be Brownian inputs in \(L^2\). Defining \(D_t=X_t-Y_t-A_t\) cancels \(B\) and \(A\) algebraically and leaves

\[D_t=-\int_0^t[F(X_s)-G_s]ds,\quad D_0=0.\]

Continuity of the integrand makes this remainder differentiable with derivative \(-F(X_t)+G_t\). Equivalently, for \(D=X-Y-A\), the remainder \(D\) is differentiable although \(A\) need not be. Since \(X-Y=D+A\), its derivative is bounded by \(L\|D\|+La+C\). Grönwall bounds \(D\), and restoring \(A\) adds \(a\). This proves the estimate using only continuous integral equations.



\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The comparison with a common inverse sampling factor}{The comparison with a common inverse sampling factor}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldForcing-lean-2}


The zero-initial-error Grönwall bound is \(C\Psi(L,T)\), where \(\Psi\) is defined above. It is linear in \(C\), including when \(L=0\). Let \(r>0\) and suppose that \(\|A_t\|\le a/r\) and \(\|F(Y_t)-G_t\|\le C/r\) throughout the interval. Substitution in the preceding bound gives

\[\|X_t-Y_t\|\le\frac{(C+La)\Psi(L,T)+a}{r}.\]

Indeed, the differential inequality for the remainder has forcing \(C/r+La/r=(C+La)/r\). Grönwall multiplies this forcing by \(\Psi(L,T)\), and restoring the additional input contributes \(a/r\). For \(r=\sqrt N\), with \(N>0\), the resulting coefficient is independent of \(N\) whenever \(C,L,a,T\) are independent of \(N\).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The product measure and Brownian evaluations}{The product measure and Brownian evaluations}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldInitialSource-lean-1}


The probability space has measure \(\mu\otimes Q_B\). Every Brownian path depends only on the second coordinate, whose projection preserves its source measure. \section{\texorpdfstring{Strong convexity after full Moreau smoothing}{Strong convexity after full Moreau smoothing}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-StrongMoreau-lean}


Full Moreau smoothing reduces the curvature by factor \(1+m\lambda\). Specifically an \(m\)-strongly convex potential has envelope curvature \(m_\lambda=m/(1+m\lambda)\) under \(m\ge0\) and \(\lambda>0\). Compare the envelope at a convex combination of inputs with the objective at the corresponding combination of proximal minimizers. Combining the potential and quadratic-penalty deficits and completing a square proves the result. Existence of those minimizers follows from finite-dimensional convexity and the lower bound; differentiability of the original potential is not required.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Quadratic identities for envelope curvature}{Quadratic identities for envelope curvature}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-StrongMoreau-lean-1}


For \(a+b=1\), inner-product expansion gives \(\|au+bv\|^2=a\|u\|^2+b\|v\|^2-ab\|u-v\|^2\). The same expansion and completion of a square give

\[m\|u\|^2+\frac{\|u-v\|^2}{\lambda}-\frac{m\|v\|^2}{1+m\lambda}=\frac{\|(1+m\lambda)u-v\|^2}{\lambda(1+m\lambda)}\ge0.\]

The denominator \(\lambda(1+m\lambda)\) is positive under \(\lambda>0\) and \(m\ge0\). Dropping the nonnegative square therefore bounds a quadratic in the input difference by the sum of a quadratic in the proximal-output difference and a quadratic in the residual difference. This is the precise coefficient needed in the convexity comparison.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Strong convexity from the proximal minimizer comparison}{Strong convexity from the proximal minimizer comparison}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-StrongMoreau-lean-2}


Let \(U\) be \(m\)-strongly convex. Its full Moreau envelope is

\[
m_\lambda=\frac{m}{1+m\lambda}\text{-strongly convex}.
\]

Use the convex combination of the two proximal minimizers as a competitor. Strong convexity of \(U\) and the quadratic-combination identity give two nonnegative deficits. Their required bound is

\[
\frac{m}{1+m\lambda}\|v\|^2
\le m\|u\|^2+\frac{\|u-v\|^2}{\lambda}.
\]

The completed-square identity proves this coefficient without differentiability of \(U\). By comparison, smoothing only \(g_2\) and adding it to an unsmoothed strongly convex \(g_1\) preserves the curvature of the latter term.



In detail, put \(p=P_\lambda(x)\), \(q=P_\lambda(y)\), and \(z=ax+by\), and test at \(ap+bq\) for \(a+b=1\). The potential deficit is \(\frac{mab}{2}\|p-q\|^2\) and the penalty deficit is \(\frac{ab}{2\lambda}\|(p-q)-(x-y)\|^2\). Their sum is at least \(\frac{ab}{2}\frac{m}{1+m\lambda}\|x-y\|^2\) because \(\lambda(1+m\lambda)\) is positive. Equivalently, with \(u=p-q\) and \(v=x-y\), the two quadratic expressions are \(m\|u\|^2\) and \(\|u-v\|^2/\lambda\); their sum is bounded below by \(m\|v\|^2/(1+m\lambda)\). This is exactly the strong-convexity correction at the original inputs.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The full-envelope curvature and admissible step}{The full-envelope curvature and admissible step}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-PIPULA-lean-1}

\section{\texorpdfstring{Exact algebraic corrections and an averaged-proximity counterexample}{Exact algebraic corrections and an averaged-proximity counterexample}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-Corrections-lean}


The examples below establish specific algebraic facts used to qualify the source arguments: the sign of a quadratic proximal expansion, scaling of a squared norm, the inverse-step factor in residual monotonicity, and failure of shared-state proximal optimality for averaged pairwise outputs. The convergence results use the finite potential-gap and summed energy inequalities proved above. Each example has only the scope stated for its own assumptions.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The second-order sign in the quadratic proximal expansion}{The second-order sign in the quadratic proximal expansion}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Corrections-lean-1}


The proximal equation \(p+\lambda\nabla g_2(p)=x\) gives a positive second-order coefficient in the scalar quadratic example:

\[
\operatorname{prox}_{u^2/2}^\lambda(x)
=\frac{x}{1+\lambda}
=x-\lambda x+\lambda^2x-\frac{\lambda^3x}{1+\lambda}.
\]

The negative coefficient printed in the source leaves error at least \(\lambda^2\) at \(x=1\) and therefore cannot be its third-order expansion. The quadratic is not globally value Lipschitz, so this calculation diagnoses the displayed algebra rather than refuting a full theorem under that assumption.



Almost-everywhere twice differentiability and the stated moment condition also do not provide the uniform integrable third-order remainder needed for the claimed order \(O(\lambda)\) for displacement of the original marginal maximizer. The proved finite-gap result gives \(\sqrt{\lambda K^2/m}\); this difference in established scope does not prove the stronger assertion false. For \(f(u)=u^2/2\) the minimizer equation is \((1+\lambda)p=x\). Division gives the exact resolvent and its positive \(\lambda^2x\) term with the displayed third-order remainder.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Squared scaling and the unit recurrence ratio}{Squared scaling and the unit recurrence ratio}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Corrections-lean-2}


Multiplication of a vector by \(N\) multiplies its squared norm by \(N^2\), by norm homogeneity and squaring. Separately, an affine recurrence with unit ratio has finite sum \(n\) and therefore a linear-in-iteration additive bound, rather than a bounded geometric sum. These identities determine the particle powers and the strict positive-denominator condition needed in an energy or transport estimate.



\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The inverse-step factor in residual monotonicity}{The inverse-step factor in residual monotonicity}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Corrections-lean-3}


Assume \(\langle x-y,p(x)-p(y)\rangle\le(1-\mu)\|x-y\|^2\) and define the residual difference by \(R(x)-R(y)=\gamma^{-1}[(x-y)-(p(x)-p(y))]\). For \(\gamma>0\), expansion gives \(\langle x-y,R(x)-R(y)\rangle\ge(\mu/\gamma)\|x-y\|^2\). Hence the strong-monotonicity coefficient is \(\mu/\gamma\) rather than \(\mu\).



Explicitly the inner product expands as \(\langle x-y,R(x)-R(y)\rangle=\gamma^{-1}(\|x-y\|^2-\langle x-y,p(x)-p(y)\rangle)\). Substitution of the proximal deficit bounds it below by \((\mu/\gamma)\|x-y\|^2\). Division by \(\gamma\) in the residual definition is the source of this inverse-step factor.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{A counterexample to shared-state proximal optimality}{A counterexample to shared-state proximal optimality}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-Corrections-lean-4}


Take \(g(\theta,x)=|\theta-x|\) and proximal scale one. The pair inputs \((1,0)\) and \((1,4)\) have proximal minimizers \((1/2,1/2)\) and \((2,3)\). Averaging their parameter components gives \((5/4,1/2,3)\). The shared-state objective is

\[\tfrac12(|\theta-x_1|+|\theta-x_2|)+\tfrac12(\theta-1)^2+\tfrac14(x_1^2+(x_2-4)^2).\]

The competitor \((1,1,3)\) has strictly smaller objective, as follows from direct absolute-value evaluation. Each pair was allowed its own proposed parameter, whereas the shared objective permits only one. Averaging the proposals need not solve that common minimization problem.



In particular the averaged output has value \(51/32\) and the feasible comparator has value \(3/2=48/32\). This strict comparison disproves the shared-state proximal identity in general. The splitting proof instead sums the valid single-pair energy inequalities and applies Jensen’s inequality to the parameter average.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Bounded fields and positive global strong monotonicity}{Bounded fields and positive global strong monotonicity}}
\label{proof-module-ProximalLangevin-PIPULAConsistency-lean}


A globally bounded vector field on a nontrivial real Hilbert space cannot have a positive global strong-monotonicity constant. The Moreau gradient of a globally Lipschitz convex function is bounded. Hence the conjunction of full-potential value Lipschitzness and a positive global proximal-residual deficit is inconsistent. The conclusion concerns that literal conjunction of assumptions; it does not apply to a decomposition in which only the nonsmooth summand is Lipschitz.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Incompatibility of boundedness and positive global monotonicity}{Incompatibility of boundedness and positive global monotonicity}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-PIPULAConsistency-lean-1}


Assume \(\|R(x)\|\le K\) and \(c\|x-y\|^2\le\langle x-y,R(x)-R(y)\rangle\) with \(c>0\). Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality give \(c\|x-y\|^2\le2K\|x-y\|\). A nonzero direction can be scaled to have length greater than \(2K/c\), contradicting this bound.



In the alternative notation \(\|b(x)\|\le K\) and \(\langle b(x)-b(y),x-y\rangle\ge q\|x-y\|^2\) with \(q>0\) imply \(q\|x-y\|\le2K\) for every distinct pair. Explicitly choose separation \(r>2K/c\). Strong monotonicity bounds the inner product below by \(cr^2\), while boundedness bounds it above by \(2Kr\). Division by \(r\) gives \(cr\le2K\). A nontrivial space has arbitrarily large such separations \(r\), so the assumptions cannot both hold. The nontrivial-space qualification is essential.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The obstruction for a globally bounded proximal residual}{The obstruction for a globally bounded proximal residual}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-PIPULAConsistency-lean-2}


Consider the reading of the source reduction in which \(g_1=0\) also applies the value-Lipschitz assumption to \(U=g_2\). Then \(U\) is globally \(K\)-Lipschitz, so its Moreau gradient \(R_\gamma=\gamma^{-1}(I-P_\gamma)\) satisfies \(\|R_\gamma(x)\|\le K\). A positive proximal deficit gives

\[
\frac{\mu}{\gamma}\|x-y\|^2
 \le\langle x-y,R_\gamma(x)-R_\gamma(y)\rangle
 \le2K\|x-y\|.
\]

The preceding obstruction contradicts these simultaneous global conditions on a nontrivial space. If the Lipschitz assumption instead concerns the nonsmooth term in an independent decomposition \(U=g_1+g_2\), it does not make the full \(U\) Lipschitz and the contradiction does not follow.



The residual \(R_\gamma=(I-P_\gamma)/\gamma\) is bounded in the first reading, while the deficit makes it strongly monotone with coefficient \(\mu/\gamma\). Under \(\mu>0\) these are incompatible. This is a conditional consistency result about the specified assumptions, not a general refutation of the source corollary. The separate full-potential PIPULA theorem assumes strong convexity and a lower bound without global value Lipschitzness.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The proposed coefficients and their sign conditions}{The proposed coefficients and their sign conditions}}

Assume additionally that the convex penalty is differentiable with Lipschitz gradient. Its proximal residual then equals the original gradient evaluated at the proximal point. The finite proximal displacement bounds the difference between original and envelope gradients by a constant times the smoothing scale. Construct both mean-field integral solutions with identical initial state and additive input. The input cancels on subtraction, and Grönwall bounds the supremum over time of their mean-square norms.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Equality of the subgradient and gradient at a differentiability point}{Equality of the subgradient and gradient at a differentiability point}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-1}


If \(s\) is a supporting subgradient, then \(x\mapsto f(x)-\langle s,x\rangle\) has a global minimum at \(p\). Differentiability makes its derivative zero, giving \(s=\nabla f(p)\). Applied to the proximal residual and envelope-gradient formula, this gives \(\nabla f^\eta(x)=\nabla f(P_\eta(x))\).



Equivalently, a support \(s\) at \(p\) minimizes \(f(x)-\langle s,x\rangle\) at \(p\), so \(s=\nabla f(p)\). The conclusion is an equality at the minimizing point, not an asymptotic approximation.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The finite gradient difference under smoothing}{The finite gradient difference under smoothing}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-2}


The gradient \(\nabla f\) is \(L_2\)-Lipschitz, and \(P_\eta\) is nonexpansive. Their composition therefore has constant \(L_2\) independently of \(\eta\). The displacement bound \(\|x-P_\eta(x)\|\le\eta K\) then gives

\[\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f^\eta(x)\|\le L_2\|x-P_\eta(x)\|\le\eta L_2K.\]

Optimality and differentiability identify \(\nabla f^\eta(x)=\nabla f(P_\eta x)\). When \(\nabla f\) is \(L_2\)-Lipschitz and \(f\) is \(K\)-Lipschitz, the same argument gives

\[\|\nabla f^\eta(x)-\nabla f(x)\|\le L_2\|P_\eta x-x\|\le\eta L_2K.\]

The envelope gradient is \(\nabla f\circ P_\eta\). Its gradient constant is bounded by \(L_2\), which can be smaller than the generic \(1/\eta\) bound. Comparison with \(\nabla f(x)\) costs at most \(L_2\|P_\eta x-x\|\le\eta L_2K\). This finite uniform defect supplies the forcing in the integral-equation comparison.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Component regularity from the sum and the smooth term}{Component regularity from the sum and the smooth term}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-3}


If \(U=f+g\) and \(f\) are differentiable, then \(g=U-f\) is differentiable. Gradient linearity gives \(\nabla g=\nabla U-\nabla f\). Taking differences at two points and the triangle inequality yields \(\operatorname{Lip}(\nabla g)\le L_U+L_f\).



Thus the constant \(L_2\) may be supplied from regularity of the original sum and the known smooth component. This gives a justified \(L_2\) without adding an unstated differentiability assumption on the remaining component.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Comparison with common additive input}{Comparison with common additive input}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-4}


Let integral solutions with drifts \(g,k\) share additive input \(B\) and have initial states \(x,y\). Assume one drift is globally Lipschitz and \(\sup_z\|g(z)-k(z)\|\le\varepsilon\). Subtraction cancels the input. Adding and subtracting one drift at the other state bounds the integrand by \(L\|Y-Z\|+\varepsilon\). Integral Grönwall gives

\[\|Y_t-Z_t\|\le e^{Lt}\|x-y\|+\varepsilon\Psi(L,t),\qquad\Psi(L,t)=\begin{cases}(e^{Lt}-1)/L,&L\ne0,\\t,&L=0.\end{cases}\]

No differentiation of \(B\) is needed. The bounded defect is integrated with factor \(\Psi\). The definition of \(\Psi\) includes zero Lipschitz constant, so the formula requires no division by zero.


\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The finite smoothing estimate for the two integral systems}{The finite smoothing estimate for the two integral systems}}
\label{report-step-ProximalLangevin-MeanFieldSmoothing-lean-5}


Assume \(g_2\) is differentiable with \(L_2\)-Lipschitz gradient, and retain convexity, a lower bound, and value Lipschitz constant \(K\). The supporting residual equals the gradient at the proximal minimizer:

\[
\nabla g_2^\eta(v)=\nabla g_2(P_\eta(v)).
\]

Nonexpansiveness of \(P_\eta\) preserves constant \(L_2\), and \(\|v-P_\eta(v)\|\le\eta K\) gives

\[
\|\nabla g_2(v)-\nabla g_2^\eta(v)\|\le\eta L_2K.
\]

If instead regularity is stated for \(U=g_1+g_2\) with constant \(L_U\) and for \(g_1\) with constant \(L_1\), the difference argument supplies \(L_2=L_U+L_1\).



Construct both Hilbert-space solutions with common initial state and continuous additive input. The averaged drifts differ uniformly by at most \(2\eta L_2K\). Their pair-gradient constant is at most \(L_1+L_2\), independent of \(\eta\). The averaging operator gives the corresponding factor two. Grönwall yields

\[
\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|Y_t-Z_t\|_{L^2}
 \le 2\eta L_2K\,\Psi(2(L_1+L_2),T),
\qquad
\Psi(a,T)=\begin{cases}(e^{aT}-1)/a,&a>0,\\T,&a=0.\end{cases}
\]

For deterministic initial parameter and latent-only Brownian input these are the mean-field systems described above. The finite bound is linear in \(\eta\). More explicitly the averaged drift constant is \(2(L_1+L_2)\) and its uniform difference is \(2\eta L_2K\). The supremum is outside the \(L^2\) norm. Proposition C.3 instead bounds the expectation of the supremum of squared errors. The square root of that quantity bounds the supremum of the \(L^2\) error norms. A bound on the expectation of the supremum of squared errors is not established here.
