The local Borel structure is made explicit for the Euclidean weighted phase space. The numerical and reference maps now share both the same innovations and the same contracting geometry.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The specified probability source cost and exact reference stationarity}{The specified probability source cost and exact reference stationarity}}


The source is precisely the constructed phase target times a fresh pair of independent standard Gaussian vectors. The specified probability source theorem supplies \(L^{2}\) coordinate representatives and proves that the first thermostat output has the target law. The constructed Hamiltonian \(L^{2}\) path therefore has the derived Gaussian velocity and Gibbs score moments. Its almost-everywhere identification with the vector flow keeps the whole local coupling intact. The integrated local-cost theorem yields the integrated cost \(b_h\), with no analytic error premise.



Exact \(O\)–Hamiltonian–\(O\) invariance on the original source is already proved. The general Markov conjugacy identity transports this invariance to the weighted target. For the weighted local cost, transforming the initial state commutes with adjoining the independent Gaussian pair. The \(L^{2}\) pushforward formula, inverse coordinate identity, and invariance of the norm under reversing a difference identify its cost with the previously bounded constructed cost. Thus the exact target invariance and the local discrepancy needed by the global transport theorem are both established for the explicit maps.



The source consists of the stationary phase and two independent thermostat noises. The verified exact transition preserves its target law. Coupling it with the numerical map using those same noises gives the finite local root-mean-square cost used by the perturbation theorem.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The Markov bound and the accumulated bias}{The Markov bound and the accumulated bias}}


Let \(r=\sqrt{1-mh/(3\gamma)}\). Strong convexity, gradient Lipschitzness, \(\gamma^2\ge4M\), and \(33\gamma^3h\le m\) give the numerical-map contraction. The preceding proofs give exact reference invariance and local cost \(b_h\). The all-couplings Markov perturbation theorem therefore applies to the independent-Gaussian laws. The coordinate-law identity proves these are exactly the original defined sampler laws pushed into the weighted geometry. The contraction-gap and local-error calculations yield

\[W_{2,E}(\mu_n,\pi)\le r^nW_{2,E}(\mu_0,\pi)+\frac{6\gamma}{m}h\sqrt d\left(4\sqrt M+\frac{Mh}{2}\right).\]

The only initial transport premise is finite weighted Wasserstein distance, as required to use a real-valued distance. The thermostat parameters satisfy their variance identity \(\delta^2+\sigma^2=1\), with \(\delta=e^{-\gamma h/2}\); choosing \(\sigma=\sqrt{1-\delta^2}\) verifies it directly. The source step-size upper bound makes the displayed \(O(h\sqrt d)\) coefficient uniform in \(h\). There is no externally supplied stationary process, Gaussian reference marginal, local coupling inequality, or convergence theorem among the hypotheses.



The one-step transport inequality has the form \(D_{n+1}\le rD_n+\delta_h\). Summing its geometric series gives \(D_n\le r^nD_0+\delta_h/(1-r)\). The scalar gap estimate converts this into the explicit dimension-dependent global bias.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Expose finite output distance for projection and mean-square use}{Expose finite output distance for projection and mean-square use}}


The same fully constructed exact comparator and integrated local cost establish that every weighted OBABO output has finite Wasserstein distance to the target whenever its initial weighted distance is finite. This theorem states the extended-real induction before conversion to the real-valued distance. It allows subsequent coordinate projections and RMS bounds to use the result without any possibility of infinity being hidden by a real-valued distance.



A finite initial discrepancy and the finite local cost imply finite distance at every numerical step. This is proved before the distance is used as a real-valued bound. Subsequent coordinate projection and root-mean-square consequences therefore apply to finite transport quantities.
