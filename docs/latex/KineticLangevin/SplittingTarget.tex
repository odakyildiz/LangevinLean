This section applies the exact corrected Verlet identity and the exact Gaussian thermostat identity to the normalized Gaussian-times-Gibbs target. Averaging a bounded test over thermostat preserves its bound. The uncorrected splitting therefore has a proved quadratic one-step stationary weak defect, and its Markov iterate accumulates at most a linear number of these defects.



Corrected Hamiltonian invariance and exact thermostat invariance yield a vanishing weak stationary error for the splitting. Its normalized quadratic moment keeps every local error integrable.


\section{\texorpdfstring{Use the thermal Gaussian innovation measure}{Use the thermal Gaussian innovation measure}}


The additive innovation measure is the pushforward of the Euclidean standard Gaussian by the coefficient \(\sigma_h=\sqrt{1-e^{-2\gamma h}}\). It is a probability measure because it is a measurable pushforward of a probability measure. The preceding OU marginal theorem identifies this exact law with the constructed convolution innovation; here it is the noise probability used by the Markov transition.



The innovation law is the exact Gaussian with thermostat’s thermal variance. It is independent of the initial phase. This independence and the fluctuation–dissipation relation are what preserve the Gaussian velocity factor, rather than a covariance calculation alone.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Normalize the exact corrected Verlet identity}{Normalize the exact corrected Verlet identity}}


The normalized target is an explicitly proved finite scalar normalization of the Hamiltonian weighted measure. A density-weighted measure, its measurable pushforward, and their sum all commute with this scalar multiplication. Scaling the earlier exact identity therefore gives \((V_h)_\#(a_h\pi)+R_\#((1-a_h)\pi)=\pi\) for the normalized target. The argument rewrites the explicit normalization, both density scalings, both pushforward scalings, and the corrected Hamiltonian Gibbs identity.



The corrected Verlet identity initially concerns an unnormalized Hamiltonian density. Its finite positive partition mass permits division to obtain an identity for the probability target. Every subsequent weak-test comparison therefore uses normalized measures.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The normalized target phase moment}{The normalized target phase moment}}


The unnormalized Hamiltonian measure already has integrable \(\|v\|^2+\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\) from first-order strong convexity and Lipschitz force growth. The explicit normalizing scalar is finite: its Gaussian factor is an ordinary finite real number and its inverse potential partition is finite because the partition is strictly positive. The argument checks these extended-real facts and applies integrability under finite scalar multiplication. Thus the rejection upper bound remains a finite target moment.



The normalized quadratic moment is computed from the independent Gaussian velocity and Gibbs position factors. Smoothness transfers the position moment to a force moment. This yields a finite constant for integrating the numerical rejection upper bound.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Preserve the target with the additive OU law}{Preserve the target with the additive OU law}}


The damping \(\delta_h=e^{-\gamma h}\) and innovation scale satisfy \(\delta_h^2+\sigma_h^2=1\). The already proved thermostat result preserves the target when driven by a fresh standard Gaussian. Mapping that Gaussian through \(\sigma_h\) changes its law to the explicit additive innovation measure. One proves the coefficient identity, maps the independent product source through this noise scaling, composes the two pushforwards, and obtains exact preservation by the additive OU transition.



The thermostat acts only on velocity and adds the independent filtered Gaussian response. The exact variance identity preserves the velocity Gaussian, while position is unchanged. Hence the product target probability is preserved by this transition.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Apply corrected invariance to the thermostat-averaged test}{Apply corrected invariance to the thermostat-averaged test}}


For \(\|F\|\le C\), define \(G(z)=\mathbb E_N[F(O_h(z,N))]\). The noise integral makes \(G\) measurable with \(\|G\|\le C\). Exact thermostat invariance gives \(\int G\,d\pi=\int F\,d\pi\), while the split transition has expectation \(\int G(V_hz)\,d\pi\). Apply the proved corrected-Verlet weak estimate to \(G\), then integrate the quadratic rejection bound. This gives \[\left\|\int F\,d(P_h\pi)-\int F\,d\pi\right\|\le2Ch^2\frac{M(2M+11)}8\int(\|v\|^2+\|\nabla f(x)\|^2)\,d\pi.\] The argument derives both expectation identities from the Markov-step integral formula, checks measurability and boundedness of the averaged test, and composes the rejection and target moment estimates.



Average a bounded test over thermostat noise before applying the corrected Hamiltonian identity. Probability averaging preserves its uniform bound. This permits the two invariant component transitions to be combined while retaining a controlled weak error for the uncorrected splitting.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Accumulate only a linear number of local defects}{Accumulate only a linear number of local defects}}


The generic Markov test theorem applies the one-step estimate to each iterated dual test, whose bound remains \(C\), and telescopes their expectations under the fixed target. Consequently the \(n\)-step splitting law has weak error at most \(n\) times the displayed quadratic defect. The argument verifies nonnegativity of the target moment and all scalar coefficients, supplies the measurable transition defined above, and instantiates the proved generic theorem. With \(h=T/n\), this error tends to zero.



Over a fixed horizon the number of steps is proportional to the inverse step length. A local weak discrepancy with a higher step power therefore has a vanishing accumulated total. The telescoping argument keeps this accumulation linear and avoids a spurious growth of the test bound.
